We've all heard the saying, "You can't teach an old dog new tricks."

I've heard it my entire life. It's one of those sayings we accept without giving it much thought. Old dogs are set in their ways. They don't learn new things. That's just how it works.

But lately I've started wondering something.

**Is it really that you can't teach an old dog new tricks, or is it that nobody bothers trying?**

And no, this isn't really about dogs.

It's about people. Specifically, the elderly, and how we increasingly treat them as though they've reached some invisible expiration date.

Think about how we deal with older people trying to learn technology. Grandma asks how to send a picture on her phone. You show her. She asks again. You show her again, this time with a little less enthusiasm. By the third time, you're frustrated, so you grab the phone, do it yourself, and hand it back.

Problem solved, right?

Except you didn't teach her a damn thing. You just demonstrated that asking you for help is an inconvenience.

And we do this constantly.

We speak more slowly to elderly people, even when there's nothing wrong with their hearing or comprehension. We make decisions for them instead of with them. We assume they won't understand something because they're old. We stop inviting them to participate because it would require a little extra patience.

And then we wonder why some elderly people become withdrawn, dependent, or reluctant to try anything new.

Here's the interesting part. Science doesn't actually support the idea that learning stops when you get old. The National Institute on Aging confirms that older adults can still learn new skills, form new memories, and improve their abilities. Sometimes they need more time, but that's a pretty significant difference from being incapable.

Even the old dog saying is bullshit. Researchers have demonstrated that older dogs remain capable of learning new behaviors, although certain tasks become more difficult with age.

So perhaps we've been confusing *slower* with *incapable* for generations.

And that distinction matters.

Imagine spending 70 or 80 years on this planet. You've worked, paid bills, solved problems, survived hardships, watched entire industries come and go, and adapted to technological changes that would have seemed like science fiction when you were a child.

Then one day somebody treats you like an idiot because you can't figure out where Apple moved a setting in [the latest iPhone update](/post/voyager-1-still-works-when-last-seasons-smart-phone-doesnt/).

Hell, half the time Apple doesn't seem to know where it put the damn thing.

But here's where this becomes more than a complaint about impatient grandchildren.

**What happens when an entire society starts treating its elderly as a burden rather than a resource?**

We live in a culture that celebrates youth. Young entrepreneurs. Young professionals. Young influencers. Everything must be faster, newer, smarter, and more efficient.

Experience used to be something we valued. Today, in some environments, it [almost feels like a liability](/post/somebody-has-to-keep-the-lights-on/).

And there's evidence this attitude has consequences. In AARP's 2025 workplace research, roughly one in five workers over 50 reported younger employees being favored for training opportunities.

Think about the absurdity of that. We assume older workers can't adapt, so we don't invest in helping them adapt, and then we use their failure to adapt as evidence that we were right all along.

That's one hell of a self-fulfilling prophecy.

Of course, aging is real. Cognitive decline is real. Dementia is real. Some elderly people genuinely need substantial assistance, and caring for them can be physically, financially, and emotionally exhausting. I'm not suggesting that every problem can be solved with a little patience and a cheerful attitude.

**But needing assistance shouldn't mean surrendering your dignity.**

And getting older shouldn't mean the world stops making an effort on your behalf.

There's an old sentiment that says you can judge a society by how it treats its elderly, its infirm, and even its dead. I think there's a great deal of wisdom in that.

Because the real measure of a society isn't how well it treats the young, healthy, productive people who can contribute something today. That's easy. Those people are useful.

**The real test is how we treat the people who need something from us without necessarily being able to give us anything in return.**

And I'm starting to wonder whether we're failing that test.

Not necessarily because we're cruel. Sometimes it's simply because we're busy. We're impatient. We're distracted. We've created a world where efficiency has become so important that taking an extra ten minutes to help someone feels like an unacceptable interruption.

But consider what that says about our priorities.

We can spend billions developing artificial intelligence capable of answering almost any question imaginable, but we can't be bothered to spend fifteen minutes teaching an elderly person how to use a smartphone.

We have more tools for education, communication, and human connection than at any point in history, yet we still manage to leave people behind.

And perhaps the most uncomfortable part of this entire conversation is that, assuming we're fortunate enough to live that long, **we're all going to become the old dog eventually.**

One day it'll be our turn to struggle with whatever ridiculous technology the younger generation takes for granted. Our turn to ask the same question twice. Our turn to need somebody to slow down and explain something.

And when that day comes, I suspect we'll be hoping somebody has a little more patience with us than we've sometimes shown others.

So maybe it's time to retire that old saying.

Maybe the problem was never that you couldn't teach an old dog new tricks.

**Maybe the problem is that we've stopped believing the old dog is worth teaching.**
