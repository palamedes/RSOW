January 6 was bad. People stormed the Capitol, fought with police officers, and disrupted Congress, and I don't support that shit and never have. You don't get to beat cops with flagpoles, smash your way into a federal building, and then call it patriotism because you're pissed off about an election.

But one thing needs saying right up front: not everyone who entered the Capitol stormed the Capitol.

We've all seen the footage of rioters breaking windows, shoving through barricades, and fighting police, and those people deserve to be held accountable for what they did. We've also seen the other footage, though. There were people walking calmly down hallways and taking pictures, some of them literally staying between the velvet ropes like they were on a guided tour, and in some cases officers stood aside, held doors, or let people pass without resistance.

None of that means everyone inside was legally permitted to be there, and it absolutely doesn't excuse the violence that happened elsewhere in the building. I'd still think the distinction should matter. Somebody smashing a window to force their way in is doing something pretty significantly different from somebody who follows a crowd through an already-open door believing the police are letting them inside.

In the political narrative that followed, those distinctions mostly disappeared. Everyone became an insurrectionist and a threat to democracy, one more member of the same supposedly unified criminal movement.

Apparently political violence in America comes with a sliding scale of outrage, depending on who's holding the matches. Have blue hair while you burn down businesses, attack a federal courthouse, and assault police officers in the name of social justice, and we'll have a national conversation about your grievances and make excuses for you. Storm the Capitol in a MAGA hat and congratulations, you're now the poster child for the collapse of American democracy.

Maybe this is a crazy thought, but how about we stop pretending violence is less violent when we agree with the politics behind it? That's the part of the January 6 conversation that has driven me absolutely nuts for years.

## Remember the Summer of Love?

Rewind to 2020, the year America apparently decided to audition for a sequel to *The Purge*.

After the murder of George Floyd, protests erupted across the country. Before somebody starts typing furiously, I'll grant that the overwhelming majority of those demonstrations were peaceful; according to the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project, more than 93% of Black Lives Matter demonstrations involved no violence or destructive activity. Good. I'm glad, because that's how protest is supposed to work.

The violent minority, that 7%, still did some extraordinary damage. Insurance industry estimates put the insured losses from the unrest following Floyd's death at between $1 billion and $2 billion, possibly more. Businesses were looted. Buildings were burned, and police officers were assaulted.

In Portland, Oregon, protesters went after the federal courthouse again and again. Night after night officers faced fireworks, projectiles, vandalism, attempted arson, and assaults, and by August 27, 2020, the Justice Department had announced federal charges against 74 people connected to crimes during the Portland demonstrations. That's a federal courthouse, attacked repeatedly over months of unrest. Now picture a group of Trump supporters spending weeks doing the same thing, throwing fireworks at police and trying to set the building on fire, and ask yourself whether we'd still be calmly talking through their legitimate grievances. We wouldn't.

Then there was Seattle, where protesters occupied several city blocks, police abandoned the East Precinct, and an autonomous protest zone emerged. Seattle Mayor Jenny Durkan famously suggested it could become a new "Summer of Love," which, well, isn't that adorable. An American city had effectively surrendered a police precinct and the streets around it, and the mayor was reminiscing about hippies and flower children. This particular Summer of Love went on to include multiple shootings and two deaths before the city finally cleared the zone on July 1, 2020. Peace and love get a lot less romantic once people start getting shot.

## And what about the White House?

This is the one that really gets me. On May 29, 2020, violent protests outside the White House got serious enough that the Secret Service moved President Donald Trump into the underground White House bunker. Think about that for a second. The sitting president of the United States had to be escorted into an emergency bunker because of violence outside his own residence.

Protesters breached outer security barriers and officers were attacked. Over the course of the unrest, more than 60 Secret Service officers and agents were injured, according to the agency, and nearby buildings were set on fire, St. John's Episcopal Church among them. If this had been some random convenience store in downtown Portland, fine. It was the White House.

It wasn't the same as January 6, and there's no evidence the protesters outside the White House were engaged in an organized effort to overturn an election or seize the presidency. Still, the president got driven underground by violent protesters while federal officers were being hurt and buildings near the White House burned, and somehow that event holds nowhere near the same place in our national political memory. I'd like to know why. Where are the endless speeches about that attack on our institutions, or the politicians demanding that every Democrat denounce the entire movement associated with those protests?

And if someone wants to tell me those were just peaceful protesters, spare me. The peaceful people weren't the problem; the violent ones were. Which brings me right back to January 6.

## Not everybody was storming the Capitol

What I've always found infuriating about the January 6 narrative is the way it lumps everybody together. People attended a political rally, and some of them marched to the Capitol. Some entered restricted areas, some broke into the building, some assaulted police officers, and others took part in coordinated efforts to obstruct the certification of the election. Those aren't the same actions, and they shouldn't automatically carry the same moral or legal consequences.

We now have extensive security footage showing just how differently people behaved inside. One particularly interesting example is the Upper West Terrace entrance, where security footage documented approximately 309 people coming through the doors during a 14-minute period. Officers were present, and at points they retreated rather than physically keeping people out. That doesn't automatically make those entries lawful, since police who are badly outnumbered may decide de-escalation is safer than fighting hundreds of people. But think about the person farther back in the crowd who didn't witness the initial violence, who walks up, sees an open door with officers standing nearby, and follows dozens of other people inside. Are we really going to pretend that person necessarily has the same criminal intent as whoever smashed through the first barricade?

In April 2022, federal Judge Trevor McFadden acquitted January 6 defendant Matthew Martin of all charges after finding reasonable doubt about whether Martin understood he wasn't permitted to enter. Officers had been standing beside the entrance as people streamed through, and the judge concluded Martin reasonably believed they were letting him in. That's a federal court ruling, not some internet conspiracy theory.

Then there's the almost absurd footage of people walking through Statuary Hall, staying between the ropes and taking pictures. Imagine supposedly participating in an organized overthrow of the United States government while carefully respecting the little velvet ropes, because God forbid you step outside the designated walking area.

Obviously, footage of peaceful behavior in one hallway doesn't erase the violence in another. Footage of violence at one entrance doesn't establish the guilt of everybody who came into the building afterward, either. By January 2025 the Justice Department reported charging more than 1,500 people in connection with January 6, including more than 600 charged with assaulting or impeding law enforcement, and even among those charged the allegations ranged from misdemeanor trespassing to serious violent felonies. I don't pretend to know the precise percentage of people who acted peacefully, but I do know that treating every participant as if they personally beat police officers is a grotesque oversimplification.

That brings me to something else that deserves to be mentioned.

## What about Ashli Babbitt?

In the chaos of January 6, Ashli Babbitt, a 35-year-old United States Air Force veteran, was shot and killed by a Capitol Police officer. She was the only person fatally shot during the riot. Three other civilians died that day from medical causes or drug intoxication, and Capitol Police Officer Brian Sicknick died the following day after suffering strokes. Those deaths are part of the historical record too, though they may not actually be directly related.

Babbitt's death deserves its own examination, because she was killed by law enforcement. She was trying to climb through a broken glass panel in a barricaded doorway leading toward the House chamber, where lawmakers were being evacuated, and the officer fired a single shot that killed her. The Justice Department investigated and declined to bring criminal charges, finding insufficient evidence to establish that the officer had unlawfully used deadly force, and Capitol Police later determined the shooting complied with department policy. Those findings matter, and given the circumstances there's a serious argument that the officer believed he was protecting members of Congress from an advancing crowd.

There's also a human being on the other side of that story, an unarmed American veteran who made a terrible decision, ended up in a dangerous situation, and lost her life. I don't have to endorse what she was doing to believe her death deserves to be discussed honestly.

This should be perfectly consistent across the political spectrum. When a police officer shoots and kills an unarmed person, I want the circumstances investigated thoroughly and transparently, and I don't care whether that person was a Trump supporter, a Black Lives Matter protester, or somebody who voted for the Green Party because Mercury was in retrograde. The same standards should apply to all of them. We don't condemn every Black Lives Matter protester because somebody burned down a business, and we shouldn't condemn every Trump supporter because somebody stormed the Capitol.

None of this makes the two events a perfect match, and I know it. January 6 directly interrupted the constitutional process of certifying a presidential election, and roughly 140 police officers were assaulted. That's serious. Pretending otherwise is ridiculous. But one event having a uniquely significant constitutional consequence doesn't magically erase every other example of political violence, and you can recognize January 6 as a serious attack without pretending 2020 was a nationwide celebration of peaceful civic engagement.

## Then there's the FBI question

Just when you thought January 6 couldn't get any more politically radioactive, along came the Justice Department's own inspector general. In December 2024, the inspector general confirmed that 26 FBI confidential human sources were in Washington, D.C., in connection with January 6 events. Four of them entered the Capitol and another 13 entered restricted Capitol grounds, which puts 17 FBI informants inside the restricted areas that were central to the government's prosecutions. None of them had been prosecuted at the time of the report.

I'm not saying the FBI orchestrated January 6, and the report itself found no evidence that undercover FBI employees participated in the crowds or that the bureau directed informants to incite violence or break the law. Only three of those informants had been specifically tasked with reporting on particular subjects who might attend. I think those distinctions matter. What the report doesn't settle is what all these informants were actually doing there, or why some of them went into restricted areas, or why none of them were prosecuted, and I'd also like to know whether anyone ever properly examined the FBI's failures in intelligence-gathering and preparation.

And while we're asking questions, what the hell was going on with Ray Epps?

The night before January 6, the former Marine was caught on video telling a crowd they needed to go into the Capitol. He also told them to do it peacefully (that's an important detail), but the next day there he was near the front of the crowd as protesters pushed through police barricades. Nobody ever caught Epps beating a police officer or smashing windows. What made him one of the most controversial figures in the whole January 6 investigation was that he'd been out in public encouraging people to go into the Capitol before the riot had even started.

At first the FBI listed him as someone it wanted to question. He cooperated, and for more than two years he wasn't charged with anything. Then in September 2023 he pleaded guilty to one misdemeanor count of disorderly or disruptive conduct in restricted grounds, and in January 2024 he was sentenced to a year of probation, 100 hours of community service, and $500 in restitution, with no prison time. Set that next to the enormous variation in how January 6 cases were prosecuted and sentenced, where some people got probation for relatively minor conduct and others went to prison for serious criminal offenses. The facts of individual cases matter, but so does understanding how prosecutors reached their decisions.

Epps also became the subject of allegations that he was secretly working for the FBI. Federal prosecutors explicitly denied it, and no credible evidence has established that he was an informant or a federal provocateur. His attorneys pointed out that he never entered the Capitol and tried repeatedly to calm confrontations with police, and those facts help explain how his case turned out.

I still think the Epps case is worth discussing, though. When a man is caught on video urging people to enter the Capitol, appears near an early confrontation with police, and ends up with a comparatively light sentence, it's entirely reasonable to ask how prosecutors weighed his conduct against that of other defendants. That isn't proof he was working for the government or proof of a conspiracy, and it certainly isn't a license to accuse a man of being a federal agent without evidence. It's a question about consistency and transparency.

The inspector general did find problems with how the FBI coordinated intelligence ahead of January 6. That doesn't prove some grand federal conspiracy either. It's still not something to wave off just because asking about it is politically inconvenient, and if we actually care about transparency and accountability, we have to care about them when the answers might embarrass the government, which is exactly when they count the most.

## Stop pretending either side has clean hands

This is where I'm going to annoy some Republicans too, because the Democrats didn't get a monopoly on hypocrisy.

In January 2025, Trump issued sweeping pardons to January 6 defendants, including people convicted of violent offenses. I understand the argument that some prosecutions or sentences may have been excessive, but I don't support giving someone a free pass for attacking a police officer because he happens to support my preferred candidate. That's bullshit too.

It's also not true that nobody was prosecuted for the 2020 riots. People were arrested, charged, and convicted, and the Justice Department pursued numerous cases. So my argument isn't that every left-wing rioter walked free while every January 6 defendant got thrown into a dungeon, which would be a convenient story but not an accurate one.

The deeper problem is that we've turned political violence into something we judge by partisan loyalty. When the other side does it, it's terrorism and insurrection and an existential threat to democracy. Let our side do the same thing and suddenly we're all supposed to be understanding about it and consider the context and the history, ideally after sitting through a graduate-level seminar on the root causes of civil unrest. That double standard is dishonest, and worse than that it's dangerous, because once people start believing violence is justified as long as their cause is righteous enough, we've abandoned the very principle that lets a free society function. Portland is no excuse for January 6, and I'd say the same to anyone using January 6 to excuse Portland. We should stop making excuses for either one.

## Principles aren't supposed to change with elections

I'm tired of January 6 getting swung like a club at millions of Americans who committed absolutely no crime. I'm just as tired of people pretending the violence of 2020 didn't happen, or somehow doesn't count, because it was attached to a cause they supported.

If you look the other way when your own side does it, you've given up any claim to moral superiority, and the same goes for demanding accountability from your opponents while inventing excuses for your allies. Treat political violence as acceptable whenever it's convenient and you sure as hell don't get to lecture me about defending democracy.

If we're going to condemn attacks on police officers, then condemn all of them, and if we're going to defend peaceful protest, defend it for everybody. Judge the people who commit violence by what they actually did, not by which party they support. None of that should be controversial, though apparently in modern America expecting people to hold consistent principles is asking a little too much. When your definition of political violence moves depending on which party is committing it, what you've got is a team rather than principles, and I'm getting really fucking tired of being told to cheer for one team while pretending the other team's crimes are the only ones that count.
