At some point, we apparently decided that teaching history meant [putting all of human civilization on trial](/post/no-we-dont-live-on-stolen-land/), identifying the oppressors, identifying the victims, and making sure everyone knew which side they were supposed to hate.

And then we called it education.

Look at the culture young people are growing up in. Instant gratification. Live your best life. You do you. Be whoever you want to be. Your feelings are your truth. And God forbid somebody tells you something you don't want to hear.

Now combine that with a version of history increasingly filtered through one particular worldview: **[oppressor versus oppressed](/post/the-communist-manifesto-is-not-about-community/).**

Ask a young person what they remember from history class. Hitler? Sure. Slavery? Absolutely. The civil rights movement? Of course.

And every one of those subjects deserves to be taught.

But here's my question: **What else did they learn?**

Did they learn about the extraordinary scientific discoveries that transformed civilization? The Enlightenment? The Industrial Revolution? The philosophical ideas that shaped modern democracy? The incredible achievements of people across thousands of years and dozens of civilizations? Civics?!

Or did they mostly come away with a collection of historical grievances and a list of people they're supposed to blame?

Now, before somebody starts screaming, I'm not suggesting that every school teaches this way. They don't. Nor am I suggesting we should stop teaching the ugly parts of history.

Quite the opposite. I want **all of it** taught.

Which brings me to slavery.

Britain was deeply involved in the transatlantic slave trade. British merchants made fortunes trafficking human beings, and the country absolutely deserves historical scrutiny for that.

But Britain also abolished its slave trade in 1807 and passed legislation abolishing slavery throughout most of its empire in 1833. The Royal Navy subsequently spent decades intercepting slave ships, liberating approximately 150,000 Africans.

That's history too.

And what about the trans-Saharan, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean slave trades? Those networks operated for more than a thousand years, transporting millions of enslaved Africans long before and alongside the Atlantic trade.

Shouldn't students know about those?

Of course they should. Not because one atrocity excuses another, but because **slavery was a human institution, practiced across civilizations, not a uniquely white invention.**

The history of slavery is also the history of people fighting against it. Black abolitionists, white abolitionists, escaped slaves, religious reformers, political activists, and ordinary people who decided that something humanity had tolerated for centuries was morally unacceptable.

How the hell do you tell that story honestly if you leave half the characters out?

And the same problem extends beyond slavery.

Black history isn't simply a story of suffering under white oppression. It's also a story of achievement, invention, culture, entrepreneurship, intellectual accomplishment, and extraordinary human determination.

Women weren't simply helpless victims until men finally granted them permission to participate in society. They were inventors, writers, entrepreneurs, political organizers, scientists, and reformers who actively helped build the world we inhabit.

Reducing these people to their victimhood doesn't honor them. It diminishes them.

Then there's literature. Shakespeare, Dickens, Kipling, Wordsworth. Writers whose works have survived generations because they offer something worth understanding, even when their ideas or circumstances make modern readers uncomfortable.

We should absolutely study writers from other cultures and backgrounds. But why does broadening our understanding sometimes seem to require treating what came before as something that needs to be apologized for?

**Education should expand our understanding of civilization, not shrink it to fit somebody's ideological preferences.**

And here's where I think things become genuinely dangerous.

When young people are taught, whether in classrooms, online, or through the broader culture, to view everything through the framework of oppressor and oppressed, they begin applying that same framework to the world around them.

Every disagreement becomes a power struggle. Every difference becomes evidence of injustice. Every personal disappointment becomes somebody else's fault. Every time someone counters or even just questions your narrative, you take it as a personal attack.

And social media pours gasoline on the whole damn thing.

Why bother understanding a complicated historical event when a thirty-second TikTok can tell you who the villain was? Why wrestle with uncomfortable facts when an algorithm can deliver an endless supply of people confirming what you already believe?

Then we wonder why young people seem increasingly polarized.

I don't think Gen Z is inherently stupid, and I certainly don't think Gen Alpha is doomed. I think we're handing them incredibly sophisticated technology while encouraging an increasingly unsophisticated understanding of humanity.

And if we don't correct that, Gen Alpha may inherit something even worse.

The tragedy is that history, properly taught, is one of the best defenses against this kind of thinking.

History teaches us that good people can do terrible things, terrible people can accomplish extraordinary things, entire civilizations can advance humanity while simultaneously committing atrocities, and the people we celebrate today were often complicated, flawed individuals.

History should make us humble. It should make us skeptical of easy answers. It should teach us that humanity is capable of both astonishing cruelty and extraordinary greatness.

Instead, too often, we flatten the entire human story and all its complexities into a narrative of oppressor and oppressed, leaving young people unable to see the world in any other terms.

And perhaps that's the greatest irony of all.

**We claim to be teaching young people to think critically while handing them a worldview that [does their thinking for them](/post/stupidity-is-winning-and-now-it-has-artificial-intelligence/).**

That's not education.

**That's indoctrination with a history textbook.**
