My father lives entirely on Social Security.

He wasn't taught how to save for retirement, how to invest, or how to build wealth for his later years. And frankly, my generation wasn't taught much about it either. We were told to work hard, [pay our taxes](/post/taxing-people-for-earning-money/), and trust that Social Security would be there when we needed it.

Except my generation grew up hearing something else, too.

*Don't count on Social Security. It probably won't be there when you retire.*

Well, isn't that a hell of a deal?

You spend your entire working life paying into a retirement system that you're simultaneously being warned not to depend on.

And now the government's own numbers are proving those concerns weren't entirely unfounded.

According to the 2026 Social Security Trustees Report, the retirement trust fund is projected to exhaust its reserves in 2032. After that, incoming revenue would cover only about 78% of promised benefits unless Congress does something.

But here's the part nobody seems particularly interested in discussing.

**Social Security isn't really a retirement savings account. It's a generational transfer system.**

When you pay Social Security taxes, that money doesn't go into an account with your name on it. It largely goes straight out the door to pay people who are already retired.

In 1960, there were approximately 5.1 workers supporting every Social Security beneficiary. Today, it's about 2.6.

People are living longer, birth rates have fallen, and fewer workers are supporting each beneficiary.

Meanwhile, the government spent decades borrowing Social Security surpluses through Treasury securities. Those are legitimate debts, but redeeming them ultimately requires money from the federal government.

So who's picking up the check?

Future taxpayers, of course.

And this brings me to something I think is even more important.

**We have done an absolutely terrible job teaching young people to prepare for their own futures.**

At 21 years old, retirement might as well be a thousand years away. Sixty-five? Are you kidding me? That's practically another lifetime.

You've got cars to buy, vacations to take, video games to play, and things you want to do right now.

Retirement?

That's Future Jason's problem.

And frankly, fuck that guy. I've got things to do.

Except here's the problem.

One morning you wake up, you're 45. Then 55. And suddenly you realize something horrifying.

**You ARE Future Jason. Welcome to my wake-up call.**

And that irresponsible little bastard from 30 years ago left you holding the bag.

We need to teach young people that the most valuable investment asset they have isn't money. It's time. A 21-year-old investing even modest amounts has decades for compound growth to work its magic.

(Just 10%, seriously. Every single paycheck after you turn 21, save 10% of it and put it into a 401(k)/IRA or some other investment vehicle. If you can live on 100% of your salary, then you can live on 90% of your salary, so take that 10% and sock it away for later in life.)

That's an extraordinary opportunity, and we barely teach it.

Not everyone can afford to save aggressively. I understand that. But shouldn't we at least teach people why starting early matters?

Which brings me back to Social Security.

Why aren't we having a serious conversation about privatizing at least part of the system? Allowing younger workers to build retirement accounts they actually own, invest, and potentially pass on to their children?

Yes, there are risks. Markets fluctuate. And transitioning would be enormously expensive because current retirees still need to be paid.

**I'm certainly not suggesting we abandon people like my father.** Those promises were made, and they should be honored.

But that doesn't mean we should keep making the same promises under a financing structure that's becoming increasingly difficult to sustain.

Maybe the answer is a hybrid system. A guaranteed safety net combined with personally owned retirement investments.

At the very least, shouldn't we be willing to discuss it?

Because here's the uncomfortable question.

**When does taking care of our elders become an obligation to sacrifice the financial future of our children?** (And to be clear, [taking care of our elders is important](/post/maybe-the-old-dog-wasnt-the-problem/). We should do it.)

But we've spent generations treating tomorrow as somebody else's problem. Government borrows from tomorrow. Individuals put off saving until tomorrow. Politicians promise they'll fix everything tomorrow.

And tomorrow keeps getting closer.

We need to teach financial responsibility, encourage personal wealth building, and rethink a retirement system that increasingly depends on the next generation carrying the previous one's obligations.

My father deserves the retirement he was promised. My generation deserves a fighting chance at ours. And our children deserve something better than an increasingly expensive bill.

**Because eventually tomorrow shows up. And Future Jason is pretty pissed off about the mess we've left him.**
