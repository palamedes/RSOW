There's a line in *Highlander* where the Kurgan says, "It's better to burn out than to fade away." It's a hell of a line and it sounds great in a movie. In real life I'm not so sure.

On August 10, 2018, Richard "Beebo" Russell, a 28-year-old ground crew worker at Seattle-Tacoma International Airport, climbed into an empty Horizon Air Q400 passenger aircraft and took off. He wasn't a pilot. He'd never had any formal flight training. He was just a guy who worked around airplanes and apparently knew enough to get one off the ground, and that evening he decided he was going flying.

And holy shit, did he fly. For a little over an hour he was up over the Pacific Northwest looking at the mountains and the water, chatting with air traffic controllers like it was nothing, and at one point pulling off a maneuver so astonishing it looked like it came out of an airshow. Fighter jets got scrambled. Controllers worked desperately to bring him home.

Watch the footage without knowing how it ends and it's almost impossible not to be amazed. That's the problem, though, because Richard wasn't having the adventure of a lifetime. It was the last hour of his life, and when you listen to the radio traffic the whole thing becomes something very different.

An air traffic controller asked him at one point whether he was comfortable flying the plane.

> "Hell yeah, it's a blast. I've played video games before..."

He joked about Alaska Airlines maybe hiring him as a pilot if he managed to land the thing. He admired the mountains.

> "Have you been to the Olympics? These guys are gorgeous."

Think about that for a second. Here's a man who has never been a pilot, flying a commercial aircraft around Washington State, enjoying the scenery and cracking jokes with the people who are desperately trying to help him. It's almost funny until it isn't, because tucked in between the lighthearted stuff were moments where something much darker came through.

> "This is probably like jail time for life, huh?"

And then the words that really got to me:

> "I've got a lot of people that care about me."

He knew people loved him and he knew they'd be devastated. He apologized for what he'd done. Then he called himself a broken guy with a few screws loose, and Jesus Christ, how do you hear that and not feel it?

I've listened to those recordings. The voice on them isn't some fearless rebel giving the world the middle finger; it's a man trying to keep up a normal conversation while carrying something terribly painful inside, and it damn near brought me to tears.

I'm a former police officer, and I've known people who struggled with some incredibly dark thoughts. I've seen what mental illness and suicidal ideation can do to a person, and I've seen the devastation left behind when someone loses that battle. One thing I learned from all of it is that people who are hurting don't always look like people who are hurting. Sometimes they're funny, or friendly, or they're the guy at work who always has a joke ready. And sometimes they're the guy flying a stolen airplane around Mount Rainier, marveling at how beautiful the world looks from up there.

Nobody knows everything that was going on in Richard's head. Investigators never pinned down a single clear motivation, and I won't pretend an hour of radio traffic qualifies me to diagnose the man. I can still hear the pain and the regret, though. I can also hear the people on the other end of that radio doing everything they could to keep him talking and get him back on the ground safely.

None of this is me defending what he did. He stole an airplane, he endangered other people, he caused an enormous amount of damage, and it could have been far worse. His death wasn't some glorious act of freedom or rebellion either, and I'm not going to pretend it was. The medical examiner ruled it a suicide. He was the only person killed, and thank God nobody else died that night.

What bothers me is how we remember the story. The internet gave him a crown, and a name to go with it: Sky King. There were tribute videos and people celebrating his flying until he'd turned into a legend. I get why, because what happened in the air was extraordinary. I just wonder how many of those people stopped watching the airplane long enough to really listen to the man.

A legend isn't what his family got. They lost Richard. His wife lost her husband, his parents lost their son, his friends lost somebody they cared about, and all the spectacular flying in the world doesn't make that any less heartbreaking.

There's another piece of this we need to talk about, especially as men. We've built a culture that expects men to absorb pain, handle their problems, keep everybody else comfortable and just carry on. Be strong, be a provider, figure it out. And when something inside starts coming apart, well, you're supposed to figure that out too.

Bullshit. Admitting you're struggling isn't weak, and needing help is nothing to be ashamed of. Suffering in silence until you can't take another day is not heroic in any way at all.

I wish Richard had landed that airplane and walked off it and faced whatever consequences were coming, and lived long enough to find out that whatever he was going through didn't have to be the end of his story. Maybe he'd have gotten help. Maybe things would have gotten better, and years later he'd be telling the most unbelievable story in aviation history over a beer. We'll never know, and that's the real tragedy here. Sky King crashing isn't what gets me so much as the fact that Richard Russell never got another chance.

So if you're reading this from a dark place, please hear me. You don't have to be okay today. You don't need the answers, and you don't need your whole life figured out before tomorrow morning; all you have to do is give tomorrow a chance. Talk to somebody, ask for help, let someone sit with you through the ugly parts. You don't have to carry all of it alone.

And if you're the friend or the spouse or the parent or the coworker who notices something's not quite right, don't be afraid to ask the uncomfortable questions. Don't assume somebody's fine because they're smiling. Sometimes the person making everyone else laugh is the one who most needs somebody to listen.

That *Highlander* line still sounds cool. But after hearing Richard's voice, I'd rather see somebody live long enough to get old and complain about their knees and tell bad jokes, and then fade away surrounded by people who love them. A life doesn't need a spectacular ending to have been worth living.

To anybody out there thinking about ending theirs, I want you to know I'd rather be there to cry with you than to cry without you.

Don't do it.
