I had one of those stupid little exchanges recently that stuck with me longer than it probably should have.

We were talking about books and I mentioned I'd read something. I honestly don't remember which book, because the book stopped mattering to the conversation about thirty seconds later, when it came out that I'd actually listened to the audiobook. And there it was, that little look. You know the one.

"Oh. So you didn't *read* it. You listened to it."

Nobody was innocently clarifying which edition I'd bought. There was a tiny dollop of smugness on it, the kind people usually save for correcting your grammar, explaining that Frankenstein was actually the doctor, or letting you know that tomatoes are technically fruit. Yes, thank you. I'm aware my ears and my eyeballs are different pieces of equipment.

Apparently, though, I'd stumbled into one of the stranger corners of intellectual gatekeeping, where an audiobook somehow doesn't count as having read the book. I have questions about that. Mostly I have one: why the hell not?

I love books. I own hundreds of audiobooks and have spent literally thousands of hours listening to them, and I also read on an e-reader all the time. Usually there are two completely different books going at once, one I'm physically reading and one I'm listening to. The e-reader gets whatever time I can actually sit down and stare at a screen. The audiobook gets the rest of my life, meaning driving, walking, working around the house, working in the shop, and all the mindless tasks where my hands and eyes are busy with one thing while my brain is perfectly capable of doing something else.

So I'm not some guy standing outside a bookstore screaming that printed words are obsolete because I discovered headphones. I read books, and I also read books with my ears, which is a statement that apparently drives some people completely fucking insane.

## Congratulations, You Used Your Eyes

Somebody is already warming up their keyboard to tell me that "listening isn't technically *reading*," so let's deal with that first.

Okay. Technically, fine. Reading means looking at written symbols, interpreting them as language, and pulling meaning out of them. Listening means sound waves go into your ears and your brain interprets spoken language. Congratulations, we have successfully completed third-grade anatomy.

That isn't what anyone means by "Have you read that book?" though. Ask me if I've read *The Martian* and you are not auditing my retinal activity. You want to know if I know the book: Mark Watney, the story, what happens, the characters and the jokes and the science and the plot, and that absolutely magnificent problem-solving exercise involving potatoes and human shit.

Say you read it visually and I listened to the unabridged audiobook. We both got the same words Andy Weir wrote, in the same order, telling the same story, and the only difference is the route those words took into our skulls. You decoded the little black symbols yourself, by hand. Here's your fucking merit badge.

What's happened is that people have confused taking in a book with the mechanism that delivers it. Try that standard anywhere else.

"I watched the news this morning."\\
"No, you didn't. You streamed it on your phone."

"I talked to my mother yesterday."\\
"No, you spoke to her on the telephone. You weren't physically standing in front of her."

"I listened to Beethoven."\\
"Actually, you listened to a digital reproduction of Beethoven produced through a speaker diaphragm."

Jesus Christ, Bob. Nobody wants to have lunch with you anymore.

Language runs on shorthand. We work out what people mean from context, because we're humans and not poorly programmed database validators, and "I read that book" generally means "I consumed that book and I know what's in it." If it came in through headphones instead of off a Kindle screen, the story didn't get any less real on the way in.

## When Did Reading Become a Competitive Sport?

The part that fascinates me is the moral superiority. Sometimes there's this implication that whoever physically read the book *earned it* more. They worked harder. They sat there turning pages while their eyes went left to right thousands and thousands of times, they suffered appropriately, and therefore their experience of the story is the legitimate one.

But if the point is the story, I don't see why anyone gets bonus points for unnecessary labor. It looks a lot like leftover Puritan work ethic wandering into recreational reading, the idea that a book only does you good if it hurts. Spend six hours in a chair with a novel and congratulations, that's intellectual activity. Listen to exactly the same novel while I walk five miles, clean the boat, drive across North Carolina, and fix something in the shop, and apparently I cheated, since I consumed the same book *and* got shit done. Sorry?

Maybe that's the real problem. Culturally we've built up this romantic picture of reading, with the leather chair and the bookshelf, maybe a fireplace, a cup of coffee, rain on the window, and a physical book held thoughtfully in both hands so everyone in the room can tell you're having an Intellectual Experience. Audiobooks ruin the costume. I could be pressure-washing something while listening to Tolstoy, and there is no dignity in that whatsoever. Nobody sees me and thinks, "Ah, a man engaged with literature." What they think is that guy really needs to clean his gutters, and meanwhile I'm three chapters deep into the Napoleonic Wars.

## Humans Were Listening to Stories Before We Were Reading Them

The attitude seems especially silly once you remember that people have been telling each other stories for a hell of a lot longer than ordinary human beings have been sitting around silently reading novels.

Storytelling is ancient. Long before mass literacy or cheap books or printing presses, and a very long time before Amazon could get a paperback onto your porch by lunchtime, stories were spoken out loud. Somebody told the story and everyone else gathered around to hear it. Stories were memorized and performed and handed from one person to the next, so history, myth, religion, genealogy, lessons, warnings, and plain entertainment all moved through human voices.

I suspect most of humanity's ancestors would find it hilarious that hearing a story is now treated as some inferior modern shortcut. Audiobooks aren't a bizarre technological corruption of literature at all. In a weird way they're technology bringing us back to one of the oldest ways people have experienced stories: somebody tells you one.

We've just gotten really good at it. The storyteller can now be a professional actor in a recording studio with a $3,000 microphone, twenty different character voices, music, sound effects, and flawless editing. Which brings me to a dirty little secret, and yeah, I'm saying it: sometimes the audiobook is better.

## A Great Narrator Can Make a Book Better

Some books I'd absolutely rather read with my eyes. With others, the narration becomes part of the experience.

A really good narrator gets the timing right on the comedy and the rhythm right on the dialogue. They give characters distinct voices, and they bring accents, emotion, pauses, tension, sarcasm, exhaustion, fear, all those little cues that otherwise only exist in your imagination when you're reading the page yourself.

Somebody is going to scream that this means the narrator is "interpreting the book for you." Yes, of course they are, and so are you. You imagine how every line of dialogue sounds. When a character says something sarcastically, or angrily, or quietly, or hesitantly, you're staging that performance in your own head, and the audiobook just hands the job to another human being.

Sometimes that human being sucks at it. A bad narrator can absolutely wreck a good book, and if I get somebody with a strange cadence, an irritating voice, bizarre pauses, or the apparent emotional range of a tax form, I might drop a perfectly decent book within twenty minutes. A great narrator, on the other hand, can take an excellent book and make it an experience.

Then there are the books the author narrates, which happens a lot with memoirs and nonfiction. Now what? Am I still doing literature wrong when the person who actually wrote the damn thing is the one reading it to me? Picture it:

"I really enjoyed your book."\\
"Thank you!"\\
"I listened to the version you narrated."\\
"Oh. Then you didn't actually read it."

So now the author has participated in your cheating.

## Yes, There Are Things Reading Does Better

I'm not claiming there's literally no difference between the two formats. Of course there are differences, and there are things I strongly prefer to read visually.

Technical material is one. When I'm reading about programming I want the text in front of me, so I can look at the code, jump back three paragraphs, put two examples side by side, and stop to chew on a sentence without hunting for the rewind button. Reference material is much the same. Charts, tables, equations, footnotes, complex diagrams, legal language, anything that has me bouncing around the document over and over, all of it usually goes down easier visually.

Sometimes I also just want to read. I like it, which is why I own an e-reader, and I like sitting down with a book and getting absorbed. I know my concentration behaves differently depending on the medium, too. Some things I'll absorb better with my eyes, and in other situations listening works beautifully.

That's fine. Nobody has to declare a winner, and that's part of why the whole argument is so stupid, because I can't see why one has to cancel out the other. This isn't VHS versus Betamax. The two coexist, and in my case they coexist constantly, with one book on the e-reader and an entirely different one queued up on audio, two formats covering different parts of the day in the same brain.

## Audiobooks Create Reading Time That Otherwise Wouldn't Exist

By far the biggest practical advantage is that audiobooks make book time out of time that could never possibly have been book time.

I cannot safely read my Kindle while driving, which I feel shouldn't need any more explanation. If I walk down the road reading a novel I'll eventually end up as a hood ornament. And with a book held in front of my face, I can't do much of anything useful with my hands.

I can listen, though. So an hour-long drive becomes two hours of book consumption, a long walk becomes another chapter, and cleaning or mowing the lawn or grinding through some repetitive project turns into a story. Hours I'd otherwise lose to road noise and engines and chores, or to staring at the ass end of the car in front of me, become something interesting.

And it matters, because I've spent thousands of hours listening to audiobooks. Thousands. There is no universe where I'd have spent all those hours physically reading instead, and that's exactly the false comparison people make. In their heads, the choice looked like this: A, listen to the audiobook, or B, sit quietly in a comfortable chair and read the printed book.

No. A lot of the time the choice was:

A: Listen to the audiobook.

or

B: Interstate 40.

Without the audiobook there was no book. I was going to drive anyway, and walk, and mow the lawn, and spend a couple of hours cleaning or building or fixing something or just getting somewhere. The audiobook didn't take anything away from reading time. What it did was turn non-reading time into book time, and somehow we've decided that's a bad thing.

## "But You Don't Retain as Much"

Here's another one I hear a lot: "You don't retain as much when you listen."

Really? *You* don't retain as much when you listen? Fine. Maybe you shouldn't, then. People absorb information differently, though, and attention matters a hell of a lot more than some folks want to admit.

I can read ten pages with my brain off somewhere else entirely and suddenly realize I have no idea what just happened. My eyeballs technically did the assignment. They moved across every single word, while my brain apparently went out to lunch, and now I have to go back three pages and read the whole damned thing again. I'm not sure how that counts as superior learning just because photons were involved.

The same thing happens with an audiobook: my attention wanders, I notice, and I hit rewind. That's not fundamentally different. Sure, the medium can change how we engage with information, but the idea that looking at text automatically means comprehension and listening automatically means worse retention is nonsense.

What matters is whether you were paying attention. Did you understand it, and do you remember it? Can you discuss it, did it change how you think, did you enjoy it? Those seem like far more useful things to measure than whether the content entered your nervous system through the designated prestige hole.

## What Exactly Is the Goal Here?

I think this is where the argument loses the plot. What's a book for? I don't mean the physical object. I mean the *book*, the work. Is the point to move your eyes across printed language, or is it to get something out of one human mind and into another?

The something could be a story or an idea or an argument, a world, a piece of history, a character, a lesson, a joke, an emotion. An author takes whatever is in their head and encodes it into language. I take in that language and reconstruct it, and then some version of it exists in my head too. That's the miracle. Everything else is plumbing.

Paper delivers it. An e-reader delivers it. An audiobook delivers it. They've each got strengths and weaknesses, and yet people have promoted one delivery mechanism into the morally correct way to receive information, which raises all sorts of questions. Is a hardcover better than a paperback? Does large print count? If you crank up the font size on your Kindle, have you made it easy enough that it no longer qualifies? Where do graphic novels land, or Braille, or somebody using text-to-speech because they can't see?

Past a certain point this stops sounding like a meaningful distinction and starts sounding like a bizarre entrance exam for the Reading Club. "Well, we reviewed your application, but unfortunately your sensory pathway did not meet our standards." Fuck off.

## The Word "Read" Is Doing More Than One Job

Part of it, I think, is people getting weirdly hung up on a word. "Read" can mean the literal physical act of interpreting written symbols, sure. It can also mean consuming a written work and getting familiar with it, and you can tell from context which one somebody means.

When I say "I read a fascinating biography of Winston Churchill," what are you trying to learn from that? You're probably not fascinated by the mechanics of my pupils. You understand I'm telling you I consumed the biography.

If it matters to the conversation, I'm perfectly happy to say I listened to the audiobook, and I'm not hiding anything. I love audiobooks. I'll probably tell you who narrated it and whether they were any good. That reflexive "You mean you *listened* to it," though, doesn't give anybody useful information. It corrects a distinction that didn't matter until you decided it did, and it's linguistic hall-monitor bullshit. Yes, I listened to it, and you knew exactly what I meant, so the communication worked, language has finished its mission, and we may all go home.

## Audiobooks Didn't Make Me Read Less

Probably the funniest part, in my case, is that audiobooks never replaced reading for me. They massively increased how many books I get through.

Like I said, there are usually two going at once, one on audio and one on the e-reader. The e-reader comes out when reading fits the situation and the audiobook goes on when it doesn't, which means I run into far more books than I would if someone arbitrarily made me pick one approved way of consuming them. I'm struggling to see the bad outcome there.

If the supposed goal is to get people reading and learning and exploring ideas, finding new authors, experiencing stories, and generally spending less of their lives watching twelve-second videos of strangers yelling at each other online, you'd think we'd celebrate every possible way to get books into people's heads. Audiobooks ought to be counted as a massive win. What we've got instead are audiobook purity tests.

It reminds me of the people who complain that e-books aren't "real books." What the hell is a real book, the paper? If the power goes out and my Kindle dies, does Hemingway stop existing? Printed books are a fantastic technology. E-readers and audiobooks are too. And the printing press was new technology once, which makes me picture some fifteenth-century monk glaring at Gutenberg: "Oh, you didn't copy that book by hand? Pathetic."

## Nobody Gets a Trophy for Making Leisure More Difficult

Maybe what bothers me most is how we keep turning perfectly enjoyable things into status contests.

Coffee isn't allowed to just taste good, because you need to know which mountain the bean came from. Wine can't simply be enjoyable when there are subtle notes of blackberry, oak, and the existential despair of the vineyard owner to detect. A photograph can't just be beautiful until someone has argued about the sensor. And books, apparently, can't just be experienced. There has to be a hierarchy: printed hardcover at the top, paperback a little below, the e-reader tolerated grudgingly, and the audiobook somewhere down around watching the movie trailer and reading the Wikipedia summary.

Why, though? Nobody hands out trophies for making leisure harder. A novel isn't meant to be an endurance event, and the purpose of fiction is not to prove you can run your eyeballs continuously for fourteen hours.

If you genuinely love physical books, wonderful, go do that. Love your Kindle? Fantastic, use it. If audiobooks are your thing, put the headphones on, and if you bounce around among all three, welcome to civilization. We have options now. What a tragedy.

## I Read Books with My Ears

So yes, I read books with my ears. I read them with my eyes, too, and sometimes I'm literally reading one book while working through a different audiobook in the parts of the day when physically reading would be impossible. I love both.

They're different experiences. One format can suit a particular book better, or a particular moment. Every so often a narrator does something so good that I can't imagine having met the story any other way, and other times all I want is silence, an e-reader, and control over every pause and paragraph. Neither one has to win.

What I absolutely reject is the idea that listening to an unabridged audiobook means I didn't experience the book. You read it visually, I listened, and afterward we can sit down and talk about the same characters and ideas, the same story and ending, the same passages that stuck with us. The book arrived. Mission accomplished.

And if you still want to say, "Well, technically you didn't *read* it," then okay, you win. I listened to the book. I also understood it, enjoyed it, remember it, and can discuss it. While you were carefully moving your eyes across every page to get your literary passport stamped properly, I listened to the same damned thing while driving three hundred miles and getting where I needed to go.

But sure, you used your eyes. Congratulations on doing it the hard way.
