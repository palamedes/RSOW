At first glance, the Dead Internet Theory sounds like exactly the sort of thing you'd hear from a guy wearing a bathrobe at 3:00 in the morning while explaining that the CIA replaced his neighbor with a chatbot.

The theory, which started circulating through weird corners of the internet before breaking into wider public view around 2021, basically claimed that the internet had already died. Somewhere around 2016 or 2017, according to its believers, the web stopped being predominantly humans talking to other humans and became an elaborate synthetic ecosystem of bots, algorithms, fake accounts, manufactured trends and automated content designed to manipulate the remaining actual people.

Some versions went considerably farther and dragged governments, corporations and intelligence agencies into it, which is usually the point where most normal people rolled their eyes and moved on.

And honestly, that was probably reasonable.

The problem is that the theory may have been wrong for the right reasons.

Because while there is no evidence that the internet secretly died in 2016, we have spent the years since then building many of the exact things the theory claimed already existed.

## What Was the Dead Internet Theory Actually Saying?

The phrase really took off after a 2021 post titled *Dead Internet Theory: Most of the Internet is Fake* appeared on an obscure forum called Agora Road's Macintosh Cafe. The basic premise was that enormous portions of apparently human internet activity weren't human at all. Bots generated posts, fake accounts manufactured engagement, algorithms determined what became visible, and the humans still wandering around inside this giant digital Truman Show mistook all of that activity for genuine culture.

At the time, there was an obvious problem with the theory: where was this magical artificial intelligence supposedly generating convincing human content at enormous scale?

This was 2021. ChatGPT didn't exist. Normal people weren't generating photographs, articles, videos, music and believable conversation with AI while sitting on the toilet.

Then November 2022 happened, and suddenly the ridiculous part stopped being ridiculous.

To be clear, the internet is not literally dead. There are billions of actual human beings online, and there is no credible evidence that nearly everyone you interact with is secretly a bot or that some government supercomputer replaced the internet years ago.

That isn't the interesting argument anymore.

The interesting question is whether enough of the internet has become synthetic, automated or manipulated that **apparent online activity can no longer be trusted as evidence of actual human activity.**

That question is much harder to laugh off.

## Most Web Traffic Isn't Human Anymore

Imperva's 2025 Bad Bot Report analyzed internet traffic from 2024 and found that automated traffic accounted for **51% of all web traffic**, compared with 49% generated by humans. Bad bots alone accounted for 37%, while legitimate automation such as search crawlers made up another 14%.

Now, before somebody starts yelling that half the people on Facebook are robots, that isn't what this means. A search crawler fetching thousands of webpages counts as traffic. Security scanners count. Monitoring systems count. AI crawlers count.

Traffic is not people.

But it is still worth appreciating how casually we have crossed a fairly bizarre line. For most of the history of the internet, machines existed primarily to serve humans information. Today, human requests are the minority of requests hitting the web.

That doesn't prove the Dead Internet Theory, but it certainly makes the name feel a little less stupid.

## Then AI Started Writing the Internet

This is where things get considerably stranger.

In 2026, Pew Research Center analyzed nearly 490,000 English-language webpages collected through Common Crawl. Across the entire sample, about **10% showed significant signs of AI authorship or editing**.

Ten percent doesn't sound particularly apocalyptic until you remember that much of the internet predates modern generative AI. Obviously an article from 2007 wasn't written by ChatGPT.

When Pew narrowed the analysis to pages published after ChatGPT launched in November 2022, **more than one-third showed significant signs of AI authorship.**

Other studies have produced even larger numbers, although methodologies differ. Ahrefs analyzed 900,000 newly discovered English webpages in 2025 and estimated that roughly **74% contained at least some AI-generated material**. Importantly, only a small percentage appeared to be entirely AI-written; most looked like some mixture of human and machine work, and AI detection itself is imperfect.

That's an important distinction because the argument is not that three-quarters of the internet is fake.

The argument is that AI became involved in the production of enormous amounts of new web content almost overnight.

Another analysis from Graphite looked specifically at web articles and estimated that AI-generated articles surpassed human-written articles within its sample around November 2024. Again, different methodology, imperfect detection, specific definition of "article" and all the normal caveats.

But consider how insane that sentence would have sounded in 2019:

**Researchers estimate machines may now produce more new web articles than humans.**

And somehow we're already getting used to it.

## Welcome to the Age of AI Slop

You've almost certainly seen the symptoms.

Facebook is full of bizarre AI images showing Jesus made out of shrimp, 147-year-old grandmothers celebrating birthdays alone, veterans with seventeen fingers, impossible log cabins and children supposedly building magnificent sculptures from trash.

The captions are usually some variation of inspirational nonsense, and underneath are thousands of likes and comments saying "Amen," "Beautiful," "God bless," and "Wonderful talent."

Are those commenters human?

Some obviously are.

Are all of them?

How the hell would you know?

And that's the important distinction. The internet does not need to become entirely fake for the effect described by the Dead Internet Theory to become real.

It only needs **enough uncertainty that social proof becomes unreliable.**

## Fake Engagement Is Already Industrial Scale

Meta said that in 2024 it removed more than **100 million fake Facebook Pages involved in scripted-follow abuse**, along with more than 23 million profiles impersonating major creators. Meta openly describes spam networks coordinating fake engagement to inflate reach.

Reddit has the same problem. In July 2026, Reddit said its automated systems were revoking **nearly two million fake votes every day**.

Think about that for a second.

The entire premise of Reddit is social voting. Someone posts something, people upvote it, it rises, and other people interpret that rising score as evidence that actual humans found it interesting, useful or correct.

Except Reddit is apparently throwing out roughly two million attempts per day to artificially manipulate that mechanism.

That does not mean Reddit is fake, just as Meta removing fake accounts does not mean Facebook is fake. It means likes, shares, votes, followers and comments are increasingly poor proxies for what actual human beings believe.

And that matters because humans are social animals. We constantly look around to see what everybody else is doing. A restaurant with a line must be good. A video with ten million views must be important. An opinion with 80,000 likes must have broad support.

But what happens when "everybody" isn't everybody?

That is the real power of synthetic engagement. It doesn't necessarily need to convince you directly. It may only need to convince you that **everyone else has already been convinced.**

## AI Makes Fake People Cheap

Fake accounts are not new. What AI changes is the economics.

A convincing fake persona used to require work. Somebody had to create the profile, find a photograph, write the bio, make posts, respond to comments and keep the account alive. Managing hundreds of believable fake people required either a lot of humans or a fairly sophisticated automation operation.

AI dramatically lowers that cost.

One person can potentially operate huge numbers of synthetic identities. AI can create profile pictures, write bios, produce posts, translate them into dozens of languages, generate replies, vary writing styles, create images and even analyze which topics are generating engagement so the fake people can adjust accordingly.

This isn't theoretical. OpenAI has already documented covert influence operations using its models to generate social-media comments, create fake names and bios, translate material and even reply to their own posts in order to create the appearance of discussion.

There is an important counterpoint here: many of these operations were surprisingly ineffective. Several generated little genuine engagement because manufacturing fake people is much easier than making real people care.

That is genuinely good news.

It is also darkly hilarious that we now have documented cases of artificial intelligence writing content for fake people, who then respond to their own fake content so the fake conversation looks popular.

We have, quite literally, begun building **a gigantic machine congratulating itself.**

## Then the Algorithms Decide What You See

Most people don't really browse the internet anymore. They consume an internet assembled for them.

Facebook chooses. TikTok chooses. YouTube chooses. Google chooses. X chooses. Reddit chooses.

These systems observe what keeps you engaged and then serve more of it. They don't need some evil programmer in a control room deciding that Bob should become enraged about municipal zoning today. The algorithm just knows that anger, fear, tribalism, sex, outrage and apparently shrimp Jesus keep people staring at screens.

Now combine that recommendation system with cheap synthetic content.

A human creator might spend three days researching and producing a thoughtful article. A content farm can generate thousands of articles before lunch.

Which one gives the algorithm more opportunities to discover something that attracts clicks?

Exactly.

## We Have Industrialized Bullshit

This may ultimately matter more than bots pretending to be people.

Generative AI has driven the marginal cost of producing plausible content toward zero.

Before AI, even shitty websites required somebody to write shitty articles. Now you can tell software to produce hundreds of articles about lawn mowers, optimize them for search engines, create images, write promotional social posts and schedule the entire campaign.

The person operating the site does not need to know anything about lawn mowers.

So we have created an information economy where **bullshit is dramatically cheaper to manufacture than truth.**

Truth has overhead.

You have to research it, verify it, read documents, interview people, run experiments, visit places, take photographs and actually understand the subject.

Bullshit only has to sound plausible.

And AI is exceptionally good at plausible.

## The Old Web Economic Model Is Breaking Too

The old internet had a simple informal bargain.

I create something useful. Google indexes it. Someone searches for the subject. Google sends that person to my page. I receive traffic, which gives me some reason to keep producing information.

AI changes that equation.

An AI crawler reads the page. An AI system retrieves or absorbs the information. A user asks a question. The AI summarizes what the human creator wrote, and the user may never visit the original source.

Cloudflare has documented enormous differences between how aggressively AI companies crawl websites and how much referral traffic they send back. The exact ratios vary and referral attribution is imperfect, but the overall direction is difficult to dispute: AI systems can consume vastly more publisher content than they return in traffic.

Now follow that incentive structure forward.

Humans do the expensive work of creating original information. Machines consume it. Machines summarize it. Humans stop visiting the original creators. Those creators lose economic incentive to continue producing it.

Meanwhile, machines can cheaply generate millions of replacement pages.

That's probably not a great long-term plan.

## And Then AI Starts Eating AI

This is where things begin sounding like science fiction again.

Modern AI models learned largely from enormous collections of human-generated material: books, websites, forums, code, documentation, articles, scientific papers and conversations.

Human civilization accidentally spent decades assembling the largest training dataset in history.

AI learned from it.

Now AI is writing the internet.

So what trains the next AI?

The internet.

You can probably see where this is going.

AI trains on humans. AI creates content. That content enters the web. Another AI scrapes the web and learns from AI-generated material. Its output goes back onto the web, where another model eventually encounters it.

It's the information equivalent of photocopying a photocopy of a photocopy until grandma's face looks like weather radar.

Researchers have already demonstrated a phenomenon called **model collapse**, where models repeatedly trained on recursively generated synthetic data can gradually lose information about the underlying real-world distribution. Rare information can disappear, diversity can shrink and errors can compound.

That does not mean synthetic data is inherently bad. Carefully curated synthetic data can be extraordinarily useful.

The dangerous word is **indiscriminately**.

And the public internet is about as indiscriminate as humanity has ever managed to make anything.

## So What Happens to the Human Internet?

This is the part I find most depressing.

The internet has historically functioned as an enormous distributed record of human experience.

You could search for a product and find people who owned it. Search for a medical condition and find somebody who had experienced it. Search for a bizarre mechanical problem and find some guy named Larry on a forum in 2007 who had exactly the same problem and explains that the fucking ground wire behind cylinder six corrodes.

Larry is what made the internet magical.

Larry wasn't optimizing for engagement. Larry wasn't creating a funnel. Larry wasn't building a personal brand or trying to become an influencer.

Larry had a busted boat.

Larry fixed the boat.

Then Larry told you how he fixed the boat.

**That was the human internet.**

Now we're burying Larry under seventeen thousand AI-generated articles titled *10 Common Reasons Your Boat Won't Start — Number Seven Will Surprise You.*

We're not just creating more information.

We're changing the **signal-to-noise ratio of civilization's largest information system.**

## Proof of Humanity May Become Valuable

I suspect one of the most valuable things on the future internet may simply be knowing that an actual person made something.

Not because humans are automatically smarter. Fifteen minutes on Facebook will cure anyone of that idea.

But because knowing that another human actually experienced something has informational value.

A verified purchase has value. A photograph with provenance has value. A forum account that has existed for fifteen years has value. A community where people actually know one another has value.

We may eventually end up with two internets layered on top of each other: an effectively infinite synthetic internet where content is generated instantly and personalized to you, and a smaller human internet where authenticity itself becomes scarce.

Artisanal information.

*"This comment was written by a free-range organic human."*

We'll probably pay extra.

## And No, This Isn't an Anti-AI Rant

That would be pretty fucking hypocritical.

I'm using AI to help research and organize this article.

AI is extraordinary. It can translate languages, write software, analyze data, tutor people, generate images, discover patterns and give one person capabilities that once required entire teams.

That's precisely why the problem matters.

The internet was designed under the assumption that **creating convincing human content was expensive.**

That assumption is dead.

Creating a believable article used to require a writer. Creating a convincing photograph required a camera. Creating convincing video required actors, equipment and editing. Creating thousands of believable social-media personas required an army.

Now the cost of all of those things is collapsing.

But our systems for measuring popularity, consensus, credibility and authenticity were built for the old world.

## The Internet Doesn't Have to Be Dead to Become Haunted

So is the Dead Internet Theory true?

Not in its original maximalist form.

There is no credible evidence that nearly everybody online is fake or that the internet secretly died in 2016.

But look at the pieces we now have.

More web requests are automated than human. AI-generated material is becoming a substantial share of newly published content. Platforms remove staggering quantities of fake accounts, spam and manipulated engagement. Influence operations have already used AI to create fake personas and fake conversations. Recommendation algorithms decide which pieces of that ecosystem actual humans see. AI crawlers consume human-created knowledge, and AI-generated material is increasingly flowing back into the same ecosystem from which future systems will learn.

No shadowy supercomputer is required.

No secret meeting.

No conspiracy.

Just economics, automation, algorithms, incentives and scale.

Which may actually be creepier.

Nobody had to deliberately build the Dead Internet. We built systems that rewarded engagement, then automated engagement. We built systems that rewarded content, then automated content. We built systems that measured popularity, then automated popularity. We built systems that learned from the internet, then allowed those systems to start writing the internet they would eventually learn from.

Every individual step made perfect sense.

And at the end of all those perfectly reasonable decisions, we find ourselves staring at a screen wondering whether the article we're reading was written by a person, whether the photograph ever happened, whether the reviews were purchased, whether the comments were generated, whether the likes are real, whether the trending topic actually trended and whether the person screaming at us from the other side of the political aisle even fucking exists.

That is the part the original Dead Internet people got right.

They may have gotten the mechanism wrong. They may have gotten the timeline wrong. They may have wrapped it in conspiracy theories and intelligence agencies and whatever other weird shit you discover when you wander far enough into internet forums at three in the morning.

But they noticed something important before most of us did:

**An internet full of information isn't necessarily an internet full of people.**

Maybe the internet didn't die in 2016, and maybe it isn't dead today.

But we're rapidly creating a world where artificial evidence of human activity is cheaper to manufacture than the human activity itself.

And if machines write the articles, generate the pictures, create the accounts, post the comments, manufacture the engagement, choose what trends, summarize what was written and eventually train on the resulting internet, there may still be billions of humans online.

**We'll just be the weird minority wandering around wondering who the hell we're talking to.**
