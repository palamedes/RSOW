You know what's interesting? Ask someone if they eat veal, and there's a pretty good chance you'll get a disgusted look followed by something like, "Oh God, no! Those are BABY COWS!"

And they'll say this while eating a chicken sandwich.

Apparently, irony isn't on the menu.

For decades, veal has been treated like some uniquely evil culinary creation. People who happily eat beef, pork, and chicken will suddenly discover their deep moral convictions when somebody orders veal parmesan.

But here's a question: **Do these people actually know anything about veal, or are they just repeating something they were told thirty years ago?**

Because I decided to look into it, and the numbers are pretty damn interesting.

## Let's Talk About Those Poor Baby Cows

In the United States, most commercially produced milk-fed veal comes from calves slaughtered at around five to six months old. These animals can weigh 500 pounds or more.

Now let's compare that with a few other animals we routinely eat.

A typical chicken is slaughtered at around six to eight **weeks** old. Not months. Weeks.

Pigs? Usually around five to six months old.

And here's another interesting way to look at it. Cattle can live 15–20 years, pigs 10–20 years, and many domestic chickens 5–10 years. Using those potential lifespans, a six-month-old veal calf has lived roughly **3% of its possible life**. A six-month-old pig? Also around **3%**. A seven-week-old chicken? Roughly **2%** compared with an ordinary domestic chicken, although commercial meat chickens are bred for rapid growth and often couldn't live nearly that long without serious health problems. These are rough comparisons, but they raise an uncomfortable question: **If we're going to judge the morality of eating an animal based on how much of its potential life it got to experience, why the hell have we singled out veal?**

So the person refusing to eat veal because it's a "baby cow" might be eating a chicken that lived less than two months or bacon from a pig slaughtered at roughly the same age as the calf.

But somehow the calf is the tragedy.

Why?

Seriously. What's the moral distinction here? Is it because calves have big brown eyes? Because we've seen adorable pictures of them? Because somebody produced a particularly effective animal-rights campaign decades ago?

I'm genuinely curious.

## But What About Those Horrible Veal Crates?

Ah, yes. Now we're getting somewhere.

Historically, the veal industry had some pretty awful practices. Calves were confined in restrictive individual crates, sometimes tethered, with limited ability to move. Feeding practices were also used to produce that distinctive pale meat.

That happened. It's documented. And criticizing it was entirely justified.

**Except here's the part nobody seems to be talking about: things changed.**

The American Veal Association committed to eliminating traditional crates and transitioning to group housing. By the end of 2017, its members reported completing that transition. Funny enough, the USDA's own consumer guide to veal still says each calf gets an individual stall. Apparently the government didn't get the memo either.

Does that mean every veal producer is wonderful and every calf lives a blissful life? Of course not. There are still legitimate welfare concerns around how calves are raised and cared for.

And yes, there's also something called *bob veal*, which actually does come from calves slaughtered within their first few weeks. That market exists and deserves to be part of an honest discussion.

But according to the industry's own figures, roughly 85 percent of veal consumed in America is milk-fed veal from substantially older calves.

And while we're on the subject, what exactly is a calf? In cattle terminology, it's generally an animal under a year old. A female doesn't technically become a *cow* until she's given birth to her first calf, usually around two years of age. Before that, she's a heifer. Male cattle are bulls or steers, not cows at all.

So yes, veal comes from calves. Nobody's disputing that. But calling something a calf doesn't automatically mean it's a newborn animal barely able to stand on its own.

There's a hell of a difference between an animal that's two days old and one that's six months old, even if they're both technically calves.

In other words, the mental picture many people have of the entire veal industry is incomplete, and in some respects decades out of date.

And that's what bothers me.

## We're Not Responding to Reality. We're Responding to a Narrative.

This isn't really about veal. It's about how incredibly easy it is to convince people that something is morally reprehensible and then have that belief persist long after the underlying facts have changed.

Think about it. Somebody sees a disturbing photograph, watches an emotional documentary, or hears a compelling story. They form an opinion.

Fair enough.

But then twenty or thirty years go by, the industry changes, regulations change, production methods change, and nobody bothers updating the original opinion.

Why would they? They've already decided how they feel.

And feelings are apparently much easier to maintain than facts.

What's especially fascinating is how selective our outrage becomes.

If your argument is that animals shouldn't be slaughtered young, okay. That's a position worth discussing. But then we need to talk about chickens, pigs, lambs, and practically the entire commercial meat industry.

If your argument is about confinement, nutrition, or animal welfare, that's also perfectly legitimate. But then let's evaluate actual farming practices across the board instead of pretending veal has some exclusive franchise on cruelty.

What you don't get to do is condemn one product based on a decades-old narrative while giving everything else on your dinner plate a free pass.

Well, technically you can, people do it every day but don't confuse that with a consistent moral philosophy.

## The Bigger Problem

I don't particularly care whether you eat veal. Eat it, don't eat it, become a vegetarian, whatever. Your dinner is your business.

What interests me is how readily we accept an emotionally compelling story as permanent truth.

And once we've attached a moral judgment to something, questioning that judgment becomes almost offensive.

"Wait, you mean the thing I've been condemning for thirty years might not be exactly what I thought it was?"

Yep.

And maybe that should bother you a little.

Not because you were necessarily wrong to object in the first place, but because you might have stopped paying attention while the world kept moving.

We do this with [food](/post/maybe-salt-wasnt-the-villain-after-all/), politics, science, technology, and just about everything else. We inherit opinions, repeat them confidently, and rarely stop to ask whether the facts still support them.

**The problem isn't having convictions. The problem is treating convictions as an excuse to stop thinking.**

So the next time somebody recoils in horror because veal comes from "baby cows," I might just ask them how old they think their chicken was.

Because there's nothing wrong with having a moral objection to something.

But for God's sake, at least make sure you're objecting to reality.

**Otherwise, you're not making an informed moral decision. You're just regurgitating bullshit somebody fed you years ago.**

And unlike veal, apparently that never gets old.
