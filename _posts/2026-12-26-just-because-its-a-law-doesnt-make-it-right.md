In 1803, Chief Justice John Marshall wrote something that I think every legislator, governor, president, judge, and police officer in this country ought to have hanging on their wall.

**"An act of the legislature, repugnant to the Constitution, is void."**

That comes from *Marbury v. Madison*. It wasn't some obscure political philosopher pontificating about government over a glass of whiskey. It was the United States Supreme Court establishing one of the fundamental principles upon which our legal system operates.

**The government has limits. A novel fucking concept.**

I've been thinking a lot lately about the sheer volume of laws we have on the books and how casually we seem to accept additional restrictions on our lives without ever really questioning whether the government should be imposing those restrictions in the first place.

Take something as ridiculously simple as wearing earplugs while driving. Several states prohibit or restrict wearing devices in both ears while operating a motor vehicle. The argument, presumably, is that you need to hear what's going on around you to operate a vehicle safely.

Okay, fair enough.

**So explain deaf drivers to me.**

We issue driver's licenses to people who are completely deaf. We recognize that hearing isn't an absolute prerequisite for safely operating a motor vehicle. Yet in certain jurisdictions, someone with perfectly functional hearing can be breaking the law by deliberately reducing the amount of noise entering their ears.

Now, there's a legitimate distinction between someone who has adapted to being deaf and someone distracting themselves with headphones blasting music. But earplugs aren't necessarily headphones, and hearing protection isn't necessarily a distraction.

And this isn't some hypothetical scenario I pulled out of my ass.

In 2012, a motorcyclist named Thomas Varsel was pulled over in Ohio for wearing foam earplugs. He challenged the law and brought in a professor of hearing science who explained that the wind noise experienced while riding a motorcycle could cause permanent hearing damage.

The court acknowledged that possibility and essentially concluded that he could either stop riding a motorcycle or accept the risk of damaging his hearing.

He lost. Are you fucking kidding me?!

Thankfully, in 2020, Ohio finally changed the law to allow motorcyclists to wear hearing protection.

But apparently, it took the machinery of government years to discover that protecting your hearing might actually be a reasonable thing to do.

But this isn't really a rant about earplugs. It's about the philosophy behind making laws.

Somewhere along the way, I feel like we've fundamentally reversed the relationship between the government and the governed. **Rather than expecting the government to justify why it needs to restrict our freedom, we've increasingly found ourselves having to justify why we should be allowed to exercise it.**

Those are two extraordinarily different philosophies of government.

And the courts haven't always helped. In 1955, in *Williamson v. Lee Optical*, the Supreme Court embraced a remarkably forgiving standard for reviewing ordinary economic regulations. Under that approach, the government can enact legislation that is inconsistent, burdensome, or arguably unnecessary and still survive a constitutional challenge.

That doesn't mean every stupid law is unconstitutional. **Unfortunately, stupidity itself isn't a constitutional violation. If it were, we'd need considerably more courthouses.**

But shouldn't we demand a higher standard from the people writing these laws than simply whether they can get away with it?

I'm not advocating for anarchy. I understand why we have speed limits, traffic lights, building codes, and laws against reckless behavior. Living in a society necessarily means accepting certain restrictions to protect the rights and safety of others.

But I also think every proposed restriction ought to answer some relatively simple questions. What actual harm are we preventing? Is there evidence that this law will prevent it? Is the restriction proportionate to the problem? Could we accomplish the same thing without restricting perfectly reasonable behavior?

And perhaps most importantly, does the government have the constitutional authority to do it?

I'd also love to see legislators spend as much time reviewing and repealing existing laws as they spend inventing new ones. Passing legislation shouldn't be the measure of a politician's productivity. **Sometimes the most productive thing a legislator could do is take an eraser to the work of their predecessors.**

The Constitution wasn't written to give Americans permission to be free. It was written to establish a government and [place limitations on its power](/post/thank-god-for-the-first-amendment-britain-just-reminded-us-why/).

Freedom was never supposed to be something we had to petition the government to grant us.

**We shouldn't have to justify our freedom to the government. The government should have to justify every inch of freedom it takes from us.**
