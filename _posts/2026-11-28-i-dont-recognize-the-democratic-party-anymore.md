Watching American politics these days is deeply frustrating, and it gets worse when I look at what the Democratic Party has turned into.

I remember when you could disagree with a Democrat about taxes, government spending, immigration or religion and still have a perfectly reasonable conversation. You might have had completely different ideas about how to get somewhere, but you could at least agree on a few basic principles. Now the conversation seems to be less about what a policy actually accomplishes and more about what kind of person you must be for questioning it, and frankly, I'm getting damn tired of it.

## Let's talk about history

Few things drive me crazier than how selectively Americans are taught political history.

The Republican Party was founded in 1854, largely in opposition to the expansion of slavery, and Abraham Lincoln became its first president. Southern Democrats, meanwhile, were among the principal defenders of slavery, segregation, Jim Crow laws, poll taxes, and the literacy tests designed to keep Black Americans from voting. None of that is a conspiracy theory. **It's American history.**

Before somebody jumps in with the tired old argument that "the parties switched sides," spare me. They didn't. Political history isn't a damn shell game where Democrats get to slide their historical sins over to the Republicans and walk away with clean hands. If you want to know what actually happened, look at the congressional voting records instead of the political talking points.

Start with the **13th Amendment**, which abolished slavery in 1865. Republicans had made abolition a central part of their 1864 platform, and Lincoln personally pushed Congress to get it passed. When the House finally approved it on January 31, 1865, the vote was 119 to 56, with Republicans overwhelmingly in favor and most Democrats resisting, although sixteen House Democrats did cross party lines to support abolition.

The **14th Amendment** followed in 1866. It established birthright citizenship, due process, and equal protection under the law, and its principal architect was a Republican congressman, John Bingham. Congressional Republicans drove it through the Senate 33 to 11 and the House 120 to 32, despite fierce opposition to their Reconstruction program.

Then in 1869 came the **15th Amendment**, which prohibited denying anyone the vote based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude. It passed the House 144 to 44 and the Senate 39 to 13. Democrats resisted it and a few Republicans opposed it too, but Republican support is what carried it through Congress.

Read that again if you need to. The amendments that abolished slavery, established equal citizenship, and protected voting rights regardless of race all had Republicans as the driving political force behind them.

There's another detail from that period worth remembering. After Lincoln was assassinated, Andrew Johnson became president. Johnson was a Democrat who had run alongside Lincoln on the National Union ticket, and in 1866 he vetoed the Civil Rights Act, legislation intended to protect the citizenship and legal rights of formerly enslaved Americans. Congress, led by Republicans, overrode him. That's right: the president tried to block the bill, and Congress had to override his veto to make it law. Presidents can't veto constitutional amendments, of course, but they can certainly veto legislation, and Johnson clashed with congressional Republicans again and again over their efforts to protect freed Americans. These things actually happened, and they're recorded in congressional history.

Nor did the story end in the nineteenth century. Fast-forward to the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Southern Democrats led the filibuster against it, and Republican Senate Minority Leader Everett Dirksen played a critical role in breaking that filibuster. When the Senate voted to end debate, 27 Republicans and 44 Democrats voted to move the legislation forward. It went on to pass the Senate 73 to 27 and was signed into law by a Democratic president, Lyndon Johnson.

So yes, Democratic leaders helped advance the Civil Rights Act, and that's part of the record too. But Republican support was indispensable, and proportionally more Senate Republicans than Democrats voted for it. None of this adds up to the parties swapping identities. They didn't trade principles somewhere along the way, and they sure didn't trade responsibility for their own histories.

What I find especially frustrating about the modern debate is that Democrats regularly put race at the center of their political messaging and of policies meant to address racial inequality, while Republicans frequently argue for race-neutral standards and individual merit. My own position is pretty simple: **I don't give a shit what color your skin is.** Are you qualified? Are you competent? How do you treat other people, and what have you accomplished? That's what should matter. Whether either party consistently practices what it preaches is absolutely worth debating, but treating people as individuals rather than as representatives of racial groups shouldn't be controversial.

History should be something we examine honestly, not something we remember selectively whenever it makes our favorite party look good. Nobody gets to claim permanent moral superiority based on a party label, though right now the Democrats are making that really hard.

## What exactly are we defending today?

This is where I have some serious disagreements with the modern Democratic platform, and a lot of it comes down to the language.

When they want federal protections for abortion, they call it "reproductive freedom." I see it as ending the life of an unborn child, and I'm tired of pretending that child's life doesn't matter. They talk about "gender-affirming medical care for minors" when they oppose restrictions on puberty blockers, hormone treatments, and in some cases life-altering surgeries for children who can't independently consent to major medical procedures. Bans on certain firearms and magazines become "common-sense gun safety," which I'd call one more attempt to restrict the Second Amendment rights of law-abiding Americans. And granting legal status to people who entered or remained in this country illegally gets described as "expanding pathways to citizenship." What about the legal immigration process we already have, and the people who followed the rules, paid the fees, and waited their turn?

Supporters frame these policies in terms of bodily autonomy, civil rights, public safety, and compassion. Fine, those are legitimate subjects for debate. But there are equally legitimate questions on the other side.

At what point does protecting an unborn child become part of the conversation? How the hell did we get to where children's "autonomy" is used to justify medical decisions they can't independently consent to, and who is protecting these kids from parents, doctors, or other adults who might be pushing them toward life-altering treatments before they're old enough to understand the consequences? Why is restricting the firearms of law-abiding citizens necessarily the answer to criminal violence? And how do we keep a meaningful immigration system if enforcement becomes politically unacceptable?

None of those are ridiculous questions; they're questions about the kind of country we're building, and **dismissing the people who ask them as ignorant, hateful, or backward doesn't answer a damn one of them.**

## Compassion doesn't eliminate consequences

Immigration is a perfect example. I absolutely believe America should welcome immigrants who come here legally, contribute to society, and want to become part of this country. I also believe borders and immigration laws exist for a reason.

The Democratic Party's 2024 platform explicitly promised to secure the border. Great, wonderful, sounds fantastic. But what the hell does securing the border actually mean to them? There's an enormous difference between writing something into a political platform and supporting the enforcement policies it would take to get it done.

Look at the Laken Riley Act. In January 2025, 156 House Democrats voted against it while 46 supported it, and in the Senate, 12 Democrats joined Republicans in passing it. The law expanded mandatory detention for certain noncitizens accused of specified crimes. Opponents raised concerns about due process and the breadth of the detention requirements, and those concerns deserve to be examined, but so does the question of how we protect communities when immigration enforcement fails.

Then in August 2026 the Democratic National Committee managed to approve competing resolutions supporting both reforming ICE and abolishing it. So what's the plan? Are we strengthening immigration enforcement, handing it off to some other agency, or cutting back on enforcement and hoping expanded legal pathways and asylum reforms somehow make up the difference? Those are three very different policies with three very different sets of consequences.

If we decide the existing enforcement system is unacceptable, what replaces it? Who enforces the law, and what happens to people who ignore the process entirely? How do we tell legitimate compassion apart from policies that just shift the costs onto communities that are already struggling? **You don't solve a problem by declaring that enforcing the rules is mean, and you don't secure a border by writing the words "secure the border" in a political platform.**

## And yes, Christianity matters

America has an extraordinary Christian heritage. Christianity shaped much of our culture, our communities and charitable institutions, and generations of American thinking about moral responsibility. Our Constitution protects religious liberty rather than establishing Christianity as an official state religion, and that's a good thing, since religious freedom protects Christians too. But defending religious liberty shouldn't require pretending Christianity had nothing to do with the American experience.

I believe traditions of faith, family, community responsibility, and personal accountability are worth preserving, and I don't believe progress automatically means treating everything that came before us as an embarrassment to be thrown out. **A nation can embrace people of every faith, or of no faith at all, without becoming ashamed of its own history.**

## And then there's H.R. 7008

Here's another congressional vote worth a close look. In July 2026, the House passed H.R. 7008, the Stop Insider Trading Act, which would restrict members of Congress, their spouses, and their dependent children from buying individual stocks and require advance public notice before they sell existing holdings. It also included a separate provision requiring photo identification for federal elections.

The House vote was 232 to 198. Every voting Republican supported it, along with thirteen Democrats, while 198 Democrats voted no. Then it went to the Senate, where on September 30, 2026, senators voted on whether to advance the bill for consideration. Every Republican voted yes, and every Democrat and both independents voted no. That came to 53 in favor and 47 against, not enough to clear the Senate's 60-vote threshold.

Democratic opponents criticized the voter-ID provision, and I'd seriously like to know why proving your identity before casting a ballot is such a controversial concept. Think about who we're electing. These are the people who write our laws and spend trillions of dollars, and their decisions affect every American, yet asking voters to show who they are before choosing them is supposed to be an outrageous burden?

Their stated objection was that the bill accepted too few forms of identification and would create extra obstacles for mail-in voters, and they also argued that the stock-trading restrictions were too weak. Those are at least specific objections, which means they can be argued over and worked out: broaden the acceptable IDs, make identification free and accessible, set up practical verification procedures for absentee voters. But if you oppose this particular approach, what election-security alternative are you willing to support?

The question that inevitably comes up is whether politicians are concerned about legitimate voters being turned away or about tighter verification catching ballots that shouldn't count. Voting against this bill isn't proof that anyone plans to cheat, I'll grant that much, but demanding an answer about election security isn't some outrageous accusation either, and it's a fair thing to ask of the people responsible for writing our election laws.

I want two things, and I don't think either one should be controversial: **every eligible American should be able to vote, and every ballot should be verified as a legitimate vote.** We ought to be capable of accomplishing both.

Stock ownership in Congress is a conflict-of-interest question, and an obvious one. Federal voter ID is a separate matter entirely, about election security and access and how you'd actually carry it out. Each deserves serious consideration on its own merits, which makes me wonder why these two unrelated issues got bundled together at all. Once they're in the same bill, a single vote can be portrayed as opposition to either one, even when a member only objects to one provision. That's a flaw in the way Congress writes laws, and voters should understand it before they believe either party's account of what happened.

## The real question

I don't believe Republicans deserve a free pass either. They have plenty of failures to answer for, and Donald Trump isn't some magical substitute for constitutional principles or personal integrity, which is exactly why I want a political debate that gets past personalities.

The Democratic Party has an actual platform, with proposals on health care, wages, labor protections, taxes, education and other issues people genuinely care about, and pretending those proposals don't exist would be foolish. What I'm after is a serious accounting of the tradeoffs: what happens when a policy built to help one group ends up imposing costs or restrictions on another, or when compassion and accountability conflict, or when some ideological goal runs headfirst into constitutional rights, parental responsibility, or plain economic reality. Those questions deserve better answers than one more lecture about how terrible Trump is.

I don't need a political party to promise me a perfect world. What I need is for it to recognize that good intentions aren't enough, that laws have consequences, and that American traditions and constitutional freedoms aren't disposable obstacles in the way of whatever happens to be fashionable this decade.

**If you want to fundamentally change the country, you could at least have the decency to explain what you're changing and why you're changing it, and what happens to the rest of us when you're wrong.**
