John Cleese once asked one of the funniest and most terrifying questions I've ever heard:

*"If you're very, very stupid, how can you possibly realize that you're very, very stupid?"*

Think about that for a moment. How would you know? What mechanism inside your brain would alert you to the fact that the mechanism responsible for evaluating your intelligence is itself defective?

It's like asking a broken calculator to check its own math. According to its calculations, everything is fine.

Cleese was discussing research by psychologists David Dunning and Justin Kruger. Their work explored how people who lack competence in something can also lack the skills necessary to recognize their own incompetence.

And I think we've reached a point where this phenomenon has gone from being an amusing psychological observation to something resembling a national pastime.

**Welcome to Mount Stupid. Population: Growing.**

## The View From Mount Stupid

You've probably seen the famous illustration. Someone learns a tiny bit about a subject and immediately becomes convinced they're an expert. Then, as they learn more, they discover how little they actually understand, and their confidence falls off a cliff.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/images/2026/mount-stupid-curve.svg" alt="A graph of confidence against actual knowledge. Confidence shoots up to a peak labeled Mount Stupid (“I know everything”), crashes into the Valley of Despair (“I know nothing”), then climbs the Slope of Enlightenment to the Plateau of Sustainability (“Well, it’s complicated”)." caption="<strong>The Mount Stupid curve.</strong> A popular illustration of the idea, not a chart from the original research." %}

Technically, that graph isn't from the original Dunning–Kruger research. It's a popular metaphor, not a scientific law. But as a description of modern society?

Holy shit, does it fit.

Spend twenty minutes watching YouTube videos about economics, and suddenly you're qualified to lecture economists. Read three Facebook posts about medicine, and you've uncovered something thousands of researchers somehow missed.

Meanwhile, actual experts frequently say things like, "Well, it's complicated."

What kind of weak-ass answer is that? The guy on YouTube figured it out in two minutes!

And the evidence suggests we have a genuine problem with declining skills. In the United States, the percentage of adults performing at the lowest literacy proficiency level rose from 19% in 2017 to 28% in 2023. Recent national assessments have also shown troubling declines among students.

Does that prove everyone's intelligence is declining? No. But something is going wrong with our ability to acquire and apply knowledge.

Which is particularly absurd considering what we've invented.

## We've Created the Greatest Educational Tool in History. And We'll Probably Use It to Get Stupider.

Artificial intelligence may turn out to be the greatest educational advancement humanity has ever produced.

Think about it.

For practically the entire history of civilization, education has depended on access to teachers, books, institutions, and money. Today, you can sit in your underwear at three in the morning and have a conversation with an AI that can explain calculus, chemistry, history, programming, or practically anything else you want to learn.

Don't understand something? Ask again. Have it explain it differently. Give you examples. Quiz you. Challenge your assumptions. Walk you through a problem until you actually understand it.

**I would have fucking killed for something like this when I was a kid.**

A patient, knowledgeable tutor available twenty-four hours a day, willing to explain something fifty different ways without getting frustrated because I'm too thickheaded to understand the first forty-nine?

Are you kidding me?

AI isn't infallible, and you still need to verify what it tells you. But as a learning tool, the potential is extraordinary.

And yet, I suspect we're going to use it to make ourselves progressively stupider.

Why?

Because learning requires effort. Thinking is work. Understanding something requires struggling with it.

And now we've invented a machine that can do the struggling for us.

Why learn to write when AI can write for you? Why learn mathematics when AI can solve the problems? Why develop critical thinking skills when you can simply ask a machine what to think?

We've effectively made education optional at an unprecedented scale, while simultaneously making intellectual laziness easier than ever.

**Opt-in education. Think about how fucking ridiculous that is.**

We have the technological equivalent of the world's greatest library, filled with personal tutors, sitting in our pockets.

And we're going to use it to generate cat memes and win Facebook arguments.

## We Reward Confidence, Not Competence

Social media has already conditioned us to mistake confidence for intelligence.

Nobody wants to hear someone say, "I don't know enough about that subject to have an informed opinion."

We want certainty. Outrage. Somebody yelling that everyone else is wrong.

Algorithms reward engagement, not accuracy. And being confidently wrong often generates a hell of a lot more engagement than being thoughtfully correct.

Every generation has had idiots. The difference is that previous generations didn't give every village idiot a global broadcasting system and the ability to monetize his ignorance.

**We didn't eliminate the village idiot. We gave him a podcast.**

And politics? Jesus Christ. We've got people who couldn't explain the three branches of government lecturing everyone about constitutional law. People who have never read legislation explaining exactly what it does. People on both sides who confuse repeating their favorite commentator with independent thought.

**Somewhere along the way, we stopped valuing the ability to think and started valuing the ability to sound certain.**

And that brings us right back to Mount Stupid.

## The Most Dangerous Person Isn't the One Who Doesn't Know

[Ignorance isn't the problem](/post/its-okay-to-be-ignorant-just-dont-be-stupid/). We're all ignorant about something.

The problem is the person who doesn't know, doesn't want to know, and is absolutely convinced there's nothing left to learn.

Someone who says "I don't know" can be taught.

Someone who insists they already know everything? Good fucking luck.

And perhaps that's the real tragedy of our technological age.

Humanity has spent thousands of years developing tools to overcome our intellectual limitations. We've created libraries, universities, computers, the internet, and now artificial intelligence.

We've never had greater access to knowledge.

But access to knowledge isn't the same thing as possessing it.

And if we keep outsourcing our thinking while rewarding people for being loudly, confidently ignorant, I don't think the future looks particularly bright.

**The problem with Mount Stupid isn't that people occasionally climb it. We all do.**

**The problem is that we've built an entire civilization on its summit, installed high-speed internet, and convinced ourselves that the view from up here means we've reached the pinnacle of human intelligence.**

Meanwhile, the people who actually know how mountains work are standing at the bottom, screaming that we're about to fall off a fucking cliff.

And we're calling them idiots.
