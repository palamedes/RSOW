I didn't like Joe Biden's presidency. I thought he failed this country in numerous ways. I disagreed with his policies, his decisions, and the direction he took America. But the more I learn about what may have happened behind the scenes, the more I wonder how much of that failure belongs to Biden and how much belongs to the people surrounding him.

Because here's something I never thought I'd find myself saying: **I actually feel sorry for Joe Biden.**

Not because of his politics. But because I believe we watched an elderly man struggling with the effects of aging while the people around him continued presenting him as someone operating at the top of his game.

And apparently, some people are still pretending they didn't see anything.

In June 2025, former President Bill Clinton told CBS that he'd never seen Biden in a condition that made him question his ability to serve. He said Biden was "always on top of his brief." When asked about cognitive decline, Clinton insisted he'd never seen Biden that way.

Seriously?

Because the rest of us had televisions. We watched Biden lose his train of thought, stumble through speeches, and appear confused or disoriented at public events. No, those moments alone don't establish a medical diagnosis. But they certainly raised legitimate questions.

And yet we kept being told everything was perfectly fine.

It was like watching a political production of **"Who are you going to believe, me or your lying eyes?"**

In their book *Original Sin*, journalists Jake Tapper and Alex Thompson describe troubling incidents dating back to 2019 and 2020, with increasingly serious concerns during Biden's presidency. They report that his inner circle restricted access to him, managed his appearances, and concealed the extent of his difficulties.

Biden and his representatives dispute that account. But there's enough reporting here to ask some very uncomfortable questions.

And then there's the Robert Hur investigation.

In February 2024, Special Counsel Hur declined to prosecute Biden over his handling of classified documents. His report described Biden's memory as significantly limited and suggested a jury might view him as a sympathetic elderly man with poor memory, making it harder to prove he had knowingly broken the law.

Now, to be accurate, Hur did not declare Biden incompetent to stand trial. He concluded that the evidence was insufficient to establish guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, with Biden's memory being one consideration.

**But think about the absurdity of that situation.**

We had a federal prosecutor publicly raising serious concerns about the sitting president's memory, while his administration continued assuring Americans that he was fully capable of performing the most demanding political job on the planet.

What?!

How was that not a reason for a serious, transparent examination of his fitness to serve?

**Who knew what, and when did they know it?**

We're talking about the president of the United States. If his abilities were significantly diminished, who was making the decisions? Who was determining policy? Who was actually running the country?

But there's another question that bothers me just as much.

**Who was looking out for Joe Biden?**

This man spent more than fifty years in public service. Whatever you think of his politics, that's a lifetime of commitment. And if his family, advisers, and political allies recognized that he could no longer handle the demands of the presidency, they owed him the dignity of an honest conversation.

Instead, the reporting suggests protecting his political position became the priority.

I understand that Biden himself wanted to continue serving. He bears responsibility for his own decisions. But that doesn't excuse anyone who may have knowingly concealed serious concerns.

At what point does protecting a politician become exploiting a human being?

I'm not claiming this meets the legal definition of elder abuse. But morally? If people knowingly allowed a vulnerable elderly man to struggle in the public spotlight because they wanted to retain political power, I have a hard time calling it anything else.

And before somebody starts screaming about Democrats versus Republicans, let me make something perfectly clear.

**I'd be saying exactly the same damn thing if this were a Republican president.**

This isn't about party loyalty. It's about basic human decency and responsible government.

Which brings me to something we seriously need to discuss: age limits.

The Constitution says you must be at least 35 to become president. Apparently, we decided a minimum age was important, but never bothered establishing a maximum.

Why?

Yes, some people remain incredibly sharp well into their eighties. Others don't. Age isn't a diagnosis, and no arbitrary birthday guarantees competence. But surely we can discuss reasonable age limits, independent fitness evaluations, and safeguards that don't depend entirely on political insiders telling us everything is fine.

And those discussions shouldn't stop at the presidency. Congress deserves the same scrutiny.

We've created a political system where people can spend virtually their entire adult lives in power, surrounded by people whose careers depend on keeping them there. That's a hell of a conflict of interest when someone's health begins to fail.

I don't hate Joe Biden. I genuinely wish him well.

But I want answers from the people who surrounded him, defended his fitness, and insisted there was nothing to worry about.

Because if they knew he was struggling and deliberately hid it, they didn't just betray the American people's trust.

**They failed Joe Biden, too.**

Getting old isn't a scandal. Putting political power ahead of someone's dignity, health, and the country's interests damn well should be.
