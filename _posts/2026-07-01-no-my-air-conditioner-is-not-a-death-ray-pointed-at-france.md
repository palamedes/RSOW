Some people are now floating the idea that American air conditioning is somehow responsible for Europeans roasting during a heat wave.

And I have to say: what a beautiful little pile of sanctimonious nonsense.

Not because climate change is fake. It isn't. Not because the United States has no emissions problem. It does. Not because Americans are flawless stewards of energy. We are not.

But because this argument takes a complicated global industrial problem and turns it into a stupid cartoon where some guy in Florida setting his thermostat to 72 is apparently firing a climate laser across the Atlantic and melting Paris.

It is the kind of argument that sounds profound only if nobody is allowed to do math.

So let's do some.

According to EDGAR's 2025 greenhouse-gas report, 2024 total GHG emissions, excluding land use and forestry, were about 5.91 billion metric tons CO₂e for the United States, 378 million for France and Monaco, 3.16 billion for the EU27, and 15.54 billion for China. EDGAR also lists China, the United States, India, the EU27, Russia, and Indonesia as the world's largest 2024 emitters.

At first glance, yes, America emits a lot. Nobody serious should deny that. The United States is a massive industrial economy with over 300 million people, huge agriculture, huge logistics, huge manufacturing, huge military infrastructure, and massive transportation distances. It is not a cute little museum country where half the population can take a train ten minutes to buy a baguette.

The U.S. is enormous. That matters.

Using World Bank land-area figures, the United States is about 9.15 million square kilometers, France is about 539,000 square kilometers, the EU is about 4.0 million square kilometers, and China is about 9.39 million square kilometers.

Now flatten the comparison out by land area.

| Place | 2024 GHG emissions | Land area | Emissions per km² |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States | 5.91B tons CO₂e | 9.15M km² | ~646 tons/km² |
| France + Monaco | 378M tons CO₂e | 539K km² | ~702 tons/km² |
| EU27 | 3.16B tons CO₂e | 4.0M km² | ~792 tons/km² |
| China | 15.54B tons CO₂e | 9.39M km² | ~1,655 tons/km² |

Well would you look at that.

When you compare emissions to the physical size of the country, France is not some pristine little climate monastery being murdered by Bubba's window unit. France actually emits more greenhouse gas per square kilometer than the United States. The EU does too. China absolutely blows everyone off the chart.

That does not mean America is "clean." It means the lazy moral framing is crap.

The U.S. emits about 15.6 times what France emits, but the U.S. is about 17 times the land area. That is a pretty important detail to leave out when you are wagging your finger across the ocean.

And then there is China.

China emits about 2.6 times as much as the United States while being roughly comparable in land area. If the goal is to discuss global emissions honestly, China is not a footnote. It is the elephant sitting on the room, eating the curtains, while everyone argues about whether Americans are allowed to sleep without sweating through their sheets.

But of course, "China emits 15.5 billion tons" is not as emotionally satisfying as "Americans like air conditioning, therefore Europe is dying."

That is the problem with this entire line of argument. It is not analysis. It is theater.

It is climate blame packaged for social media. It is a moral performance for people who want a villain more than they want a solution.

And the villain, conveniently, is always the same: Americans.

Americans drive too much. Americans cool their houses too much. Americans eat wrong. Americans live wrong. Americans are the reason your train was late, your wine was warm, your radiator doesn't work, and your government spent decades pretending summers would remain polite forever.

Sorry, but no.

Europe has its own choices. France has its own policies. Paris has its own infrastructure. If a wealthy modern city is getting hit with dangerous heat and large numbers of homes, hospitals, schools, and public spaces are not adequately cooled, that is not just an American emissions problem. That is also an adaptation problem. That is a planning problem. That is a "we treated air conditioning like vulgar American barbarism until the weather stopped cooperating" problem.

You do not get to sneer at air conditioning for decades, refuse to build around the reality of hotter summers, then turn around and blame the people who installed the machines that keep their elderly from dying indoors.

That is not moral superiority. That is bad planning with better table manners.

And yes, before someone gets their organic linen pants twisted into a decorative knot, per-capita emissions are a real counterpoint. On a per-person basis, the United States is high. Higher than France. Higher than the EU. That matters. It is a fair criticism. Americans consume a lot of energy, live spread out, drive a lot, cool large homes, and generally behave like energy is cheap because, for a long time, it was.

Fine. Put that in the argument.

But do not pretend that one metric is the entire truth while all the other metrics are rude interruptions.

Total emissions matter. Per-capita emissions matter. Industrial output matters. Energy mix matters. Imports matter. Geography matters. Density matters. Weather matters. Adaptation matters. Nuclear power matters. Whether your country built houses to retain heat in winter but not survive brutal summer nights also matters.

This is not a children's book. There is not one bad dragon named American Air Conditioning.

And honestly, air conditioning is not just some frivolous luxury. It is life safety infrastructure. Heat kills people. Especially the elderly, the sick, the poor, and people trapped in dense urban heat islands. A fan and a damp towel are not a climate policy. They are what you do when the adults failed to prepare.

The answer to hotter summers is not to shame people for cooling buildings. The answer is to produce cleaner energy, build better grids, use efficient heat pumps, improve insulation, harden cities against heat, plant shade, reduce waste, and stop pretending that suffering through dangerous temperatures is a virtue.

Because that is the really deranged part of this whole debate: some people have managed to turn not dying of heat stroke into a character flaw.

Apparently being comfortable in your own home is decadence now. Apparently protecting grandma from a 100-degree apartment is colonial arrogance. Apparently the moral high ground is sweating in a brick oven while congratulating yourself for not being American.

No thanks.

America has plenty to criticize. We waste energy. We build stupidly. We drive too much. We overcool buildings. We have houses the size of minor European villages. There is a serious version of this conversation where the United States takes responsibility for its emissions and invests harder in cleaner power, better homes, better transportation, and more efficient cooling.

But "your A/C is killing Europeans" is not the serious version.

It is a bumper sticker for smug people.

It is the climate debate reduced to finger-pointing and vibes.

It is what happens when people would rather blame an American thermostat than admit that their own governments, cities, and cultural assumptions may not be ready for the world they now live in.

So yes, climate change is real. Yes, the U.S. is a major emitter. Yes, Americans should be more efficient. Yes, there is work to do.

But no, my air conditioner is not personally assassinating Europeans.

And if your climate argument requires pretending it is, your argument is not brave or sophisticated.

It is just dumb with a passport.
