## The short version

Someone makes a bold, controversial, hard-to-defend claim — the **bailey**. You push back on it. Instead of defending it, they retreat to a much safer, nearly unarguable claim — the **motte** — and act like that's what they meant the whole time.

You can't argue with the safe version, so the conversation stalls out. And once the pressure is off, the bold claim comes back, ::completely untouched::, ready for the next round.

## Where the name comes from

A motte-and-bailey castle has two parts. The **bailey** is the courtyard — the good land, where you actually want to live, with the barns and the workshops and the room to move. It's also almost impossible to defend. The **motte** is the mound — an earth hill with a keep on top, usually a timber tower: cramped, miserable, useless for daily life, and very hard to take.

When raiders show up, you fall back to the motte and wait. When they get bored and leave, you walk straight back down into the bailey.

The argument works exactly the same way. ::The bailey is the claim they want. The motte is the claim they can hold.:: The trick is treating them as if they were the same claim.

Nobody had to lie for this to happen. Both statements can be things the speaker genuinely believes. The problem is what happens between them.

## Why it doesn't work

Because the second claim was offered as a *defense* of the first one, and it isn't one.

"Corporations sometimes exploit people" is not a milder version of "capitalism is inherently evil." It's a different claim — one nearly everybody already agrees with, including most people who like capitalism just fine. Agreeing to it costs nothing and settles nothing. Same with reform and abolition: those aren't two dials on the same setting, they're two different positions.

So the objection never actually gets answered. It gets answered *at*, by something else. And because nothing was conceded and nothing was withdrawn, the bold claim survives the exchange completely intact — which is the part that makes this a move rather than a mistake.

The other cost is to the person doing it. If the strong version is never defended, it's also never tested. You can hold it for years without ever finding out whether you can support it.

## This is not a motte and bailey

Most accusations of this are wrong, so be careful. It is **not**:

- **Clarifying.** "I said that badly — here's what I actually meant" is honest and normal. Say it and move on.
- **Changing your mind.** "You're right, I overstated it" is the best possible outcome of an argument, not a foul.
- **Holding a complicated position.** Real positions have nuance and conditions. A motte and bailey isn't one complicated position; it's two simple positions sharing a coat.
- **Being asked what you meant.** "What do you mean by that?" is a question, not an accusation.

The whole difference is one thing: ::did they admit the position moved?:: If yes, it's a clarification and you should take it in good faith. If the swap happens silently, and the strong claim reappears later like nothing happened — that's the move.

## How to call it out

Don't say "that's a motte and bailey." Naming a fallacy has never once persuaded anybody; it just starts a second argument about whether the name applies. Say what it's doing instead:

- "I'll agree with that version — but it isn't what you said. Do you still hold the first one?"
- "Sure, corporations exploit people. Is that all 'capitalism is inherently evil' meant?"
- "If *that's* the claim, we don't disagree. Can we drop the stronger one, then?"

Then wait for the answer, and hold them to it. This whole thing runs on nobody keeping track of which claim is actually being defended. The moment somebody writes it down, it stops working.

And if it turns out you're the one who's been doing it — that's fine, most people have. Pick the claim you can actually defend, say that one out loud, and defend it.
