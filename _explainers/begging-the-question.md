## The short version

An argument is supposed to get you from something you already accept to something you don't. Begging the question skips that part: the reason offered *is* the conclusion, restated.

It looks like an argument. It has the right shape — a claim, a "because," a reason. But nothing is holding anything up. If you already agreed with the conclusion you'd accept the premise, and if you didn't, you won't.

## A note on the name

Almost everybody now uses "begs the question" to mean "raises the question," and that ship has sailed — it's not worth correcting anyone over. The original sense is the one on this page: an argument that *asks you to grant the very thing it's supposed to prove*. If the ambiguity is going to cause a fight, just say **circular reasoning** and move on.

## Why it's hard to spot

Because in real conversation the circle is almost never that tight. It usually runs through three or four steps, a bit of jargon, and a couple of sentences everybody nods along to, and by the time it closes you've forgotten where it started.

It's also very easy to do to yourself. ::A conclusion you hold strongly stops feeling like a conclusion and starts feeling like a premise.:: Then you reach for it as a reason for other things — including, eventually, itself. That isn't dishonesty; it's just what conviction does to the furniture in your head.

The other reason it survives: circular arguments are always *valid*. The conclusion really does follow from the premise. That's the problem. Nobody catches it by checking the logic, because the logic is fine. You catch it by checking whether the premise was ever earned.

## This is not circular reasoning

- **Definitions nobody is fighting over.** "He's a bachelor because he's an unmarried man" isn't an argument that failed; it's a definition doing its job. But a definition can also be the trick, and in its oldest form that's exactly what this fallacy is: when the word is the thing in dispute, defining your way to the verdict *is* the move. "That wasn't a layoff, it was a restructuring — and restructurings aren't layoffs" doesn't argue for anything, it packs the conclusion into a noun. Ask whether the definition is one both sides already accept, or the very thing you were supposed to be proving.
- **Axioms, stated as axioms.** Every argument bottoms out somewhere. Saying "I'm taking this as a starting point" is honest. It's only circular if the starting point is smuggled in as a conclusion.
- **Evidence that happens to sound similar.** "He's trustworthy because he returned the deposit when nobody would have known" adds a fact from outside. Different sentence, different content.
- **Repeating yourself for emphasis.** Saying the same thing twice isn't a fallacy unless the second one is being billed as the reason for the first.

## How to call it out

One question does almost all the work:

- ::"What would I have to already believe for that reason to count?"::

If the answer is "the conclusion," you're done. Say it out loud, plainly, without the Latin:

- "That's the same claim again. What's the reason underneath it?"
- "I'll take that as the conclusion. What's the evidence?"
- "Suppose I don't already think we need it. What have you got?"

That last one is the useful one, because it's not an accusation — it's an invitation. Most of the time the person does have a real reason. They just hadn't noticed they'd skipped over it.
