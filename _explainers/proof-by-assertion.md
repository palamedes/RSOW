## The short version

Somebody makes a claim. You ask why it's true. What comes back is the claim again — more emphasis, no more support, and a note of surprise that anyone needed to ask.

::No evidence enters the conversation at any point.:: The confidence is doing the job the evidence was supposed to do, and the *obviously* is there to make you feel slow for noticing.

## Where the name comes from

The older name is *ipse dixit* — "he himself said it." Cicero used it as a jab at the followers of Pythagoras, who, asked for the reasoning behind a claim, would answer that the master had said so and consider the matter closed.

The English name is blunter and more useful, because it names the whole trick in three words: the assertion is being offered as the proof.

The third is the purest version, because the word doing all the work is sitting in the original sentence. "Obviously" arrived before any argument did, and nobody ever asked it to leave.

## Why it doesn't work

"Obviously" isn't a piece of evidence. It's a rating of how much evidence the claim needs — awarded by the one person who'd otherwise have to go and get it. ::Every argument would love to grade its own homework.::

The "everybody knows" half is worse, because it sounds checkable and isn't. Everybody is never counted. In practice it means the people the speaker talks to, and it does the work of a citation while carrying none of a citation's obligations: you can't look it up, you can't find out when it was established, and you can't discover that it stopped being true in 2011.

But the reason the move survives is social rather than logical. It doesn't answer the question — it reprices it. "How do you know?" gets converted from a normal thing to say into an admission that you're behind, and most people will let it go rather than volunteer for that. ::The move doesn't win the argument. It makes the argument too expensive to have.::

And here's the honest part, because it's what the whole thing runs on: a great deal of what we say genuinely *is* common knowledge, and demanding a source for all of it would make conversation impossible. Nobody cites anything for "traffic is bad on Fridays." The move borrows its plausibility from every single time this was completely fine.

## This is not proof by assertion

- **Shared premises.** You don't need a citation for water being wet, or for the deploy having gone out on Tuesday. Not every sentence carries a footnote, and demanding one on all of them is its own kind of bad faith.
- **Stating the position before arguing it.** "I think this is a mistake. Here's why —" is a heading, not a proof. The assertion is fine. Watch what follows it.
- **Experience, named as experience.** "I've run this system for six years and it won't take that load" is attributable, checkable, and capable of being wrong. That's an argument.
- **Pointing at evidence that lives somewhere else.** "It's in the incident report, I'll send you the link" is a promise, and promises get kept or broken. Either way you find out.
- **Declining to re-run a settled argument.** "We went through this in March, the numbers are in the doc" isn't a dodge. It's a reference.

The test is what happens next. ::Ask "how do you know?" and see whether the conversation has anywhere to go.:: Real confidence can say where it came from, even when what it says turns out to be wrong. This move can only repeat itself.

## How to call it out

The whole trick is to leave them somewhere to stand. The move works by making the question feel like an insult, and if you confirm that suspicion you'll get a fight instead of a source:

- "You might be right and I might just be the one who doesn't know this. Where would I go and read about it?"
- "Who's everyone? I'm not being difficult — I want to know whose agreement we're counting."
- "Grant me that it's obvious. Obvious *from* what?"

The first one is the good one, and it's good because it's honest — sometimes you really are the one who's behind, and asking this way costs you nothing on the occasions when that's the answer. If the source exists, you've learned something and they got to be helpful. If it doesn't, you don't have to say so. Everybody in the room does that arithmetic on their own.
