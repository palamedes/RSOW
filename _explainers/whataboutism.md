## The short version

You raise a problem. Instead of addressing it, they raise a *different* problem — usually one belonging to you, your side, or somebody you like. The subject changes. The original point is never touched. And if you chase the new one, you've agreed to the trade without noticing you made it.

## Where the name comes from

The word starts in an argument about the Troubles, not about Moscow. On 30 January 1974 *The Irish Times* published a letter from Sean O'Conaill, a history teacher in Northern Ireland, complaining about "the Whatabouts" — the people who answered every condemnation of the Provisional IRA with an argument proving the greater immorality of the other side. Three days later the paper's John Healy, writing as "Backbencher", picked the letter up and gave the habit its name: ::whataboutery::.

What made it famous was the Soviet version, which is why English mostly settled on ::whataboutism::. For decades criticism of Soviet repression was answered with a counter-charge about American racism instead of a reply — a reflex remembered in the stock phrase "and you are lynching Negroes," which circulated at least as much as a joke about the tactic as an actual quotation. The older Latin name for the same shape is *tu quoque*: "you too."

Both replies might be completely true. That's what makes this work — you're not being lied to, you're being redirected.

## Why it doesn't work

Two things can both be bad. That's the entire refutation, and it's worth saying plainly because the move survives on people forgetting it.

::A criticism is a claim about the world. Hypocrisy is a fact about the speaker.:: They're different objects. Even if you are the biggest hypocrite in the building, the deploy still took production down for six hours. Nothing about your record changes what happened to the servers.

The other problem is that it can't fail. There is always something else that's also bad — always another administration, another quarter, another guy who did worse. If any criticism can be answered by naming a different one, then no criticism can ever land on anybody, which is a convenient place to end up if you'd rather not answer things.

## This is not whataboutism

- **Naming a double standard, and then answering.** "That's a fair hit — and I'd point out the same rule didn't apply last quarter" is context. The criticism still got its answer.
- **Questioning whether the rule is real.** If somebody is enforcing a standard they don't hold themselves, asking about that is an argument *about the standard*, and it's legitimate. The rule is the topic, not an escape from it.
- **Comparison for scale.** "This costs two million; the thing you signed off on costs forty" is an argument about proportion. It's on-topic and it's fair.

One question sorts all of it: ::does the original criticism still get an answer?:: If it does, the other stuff is context. If it arrived *in place of* the answer, it's a dodge.

## How to call it out

Don't argue about whether it's whataboutism. Just decline the trade and go back:

- "Maybe. We can do that one next. This one first."
- "I'll grant every word of it. Does it make the six hours not have happened?"
- "You're right that I shipped a bug. I'm asking about the deploy."

Then say the same sentence again. It feels rude and repetitive, and it is the only thing that works. The move runs on you being too polite to return to the question a third time.
