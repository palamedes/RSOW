## The short version

A word does two jobs in a single argument. The premises are true under one meaning, the conclusion follows under the other, and there is no one reading that makes both work at the same time.

::It isn't a bad argument. It's two arguments, wearing one word.::

## Where the name comes from

Aristotle put ambiguity at the head of his list of fallacies "dependent on language," which makes this the oldest entry in the collection. The Latin is *aequivocatio* — equal-calling: one name, more than one thing.

The textbook demonstrations are jokes, which tells you the shape is easy to see once it's isolated:

> The end of a thing is its perfection. Death is the end of life. Therefore death is the perfection of life.

> Nothing is better than eternal happiness. A ham sandwich is better than nothing. Therefore a ham sandwich is better than eternal happiness.

Nobody has ever been fooled by either. The reason the fallacy still matters is that outside the textbook the two meanings are close enough to feel like one meaning being emphasised differently.

## Why it doesn't work

Because validity is a property of a form, and the form isn't there. Write the argument out with the word replaced by its definition every time it appears, and a sound argument survives the operation unchanged. ::An equivocation cannot be written down.:: The moment you have to pick one definition, one half of it collapses.

What makes the live cases hard is that the two senses are usually relatives. They share a root and a feeling, so the switch reads as emphasis rather than substitution — and in ordinary conversation we slide between senses constantly without any harm at all, because nothing is resting on it. Equivocation is what that ordinary sliding does when something *is* resting on it.

And the most consequential version isn't a single argument at all. It's one word doing double duty across an entire conversation, where one side is using the technical sense and the other the popular one, and both parties believe they are disagreeing about the world. *Theory*, *bias*, *discrimination*, *free*, *natural*, *rights* — arguments about all of these run for hours on a disagreement that was never there.

## This is not equivocation

- **Words having more than one meaning.** That is simply what words are like. The fallacy needs the two senses inside one argument, load-bearing.
- **Using a technical sense and saying so.** "I mean *significant* in the statistical sense" is the opposite of the move. It's the fix.
- **Metaphor and analogy.** Signposted, understood as non-literal, and not pretending to carry the weight of the literal sense.
- **An honest dispute about a definition.** "We're clearly meaning different things by that — let's settle which" is exactly what you're supposed to do. It's how the fallacy gets dismantled, not an instance of it.

The test is mechanical, which is unusual in this collection and makes it worth remembering: ::replace the word with its definition everywhere it appears. If you need two different definitions to keep the argument standing, that's the fallacy.::

## How to call it out

- "Which sense are you using — that one or this one?"
- "Say it again without the word."
- "If we use your definition consistently all the way through, does the first part still hold?"

The middle one is the good one, and it is almost impossible to resent. Asking somebody to restate their point without the contested word sounds like an ordinary request for clarity, and it is — but an argument that depends on a word changing meaning cannot survive being made without it. Either a better version comes back, which is what you wanted, or nothing does, which everybody in the room notices at the same moment.

One relative worth naming: [motte and bailey](/explain/motte-and-bailey/) is this move with a retreat bolted on. Two senses, plus a strategy for moving between them under pressure. Equivocation is the base move it's built from.
